## What this source is

ThinkHigher’s report titled *The Black Experience Project* / Black Student Experience Report, authored by Jason Arday with Claire Muttock and Jamie Ormes listed as key contributors. The PDF is dated January 2022.

## Evidence boundary

- The report is a public-facing research/policy artifact, not a peer-reviewed journal article.
- ThinkHigher’s project page describes work with Black students in Coventry and Warwickshire; the report’s own methods and sample should be consulted before generalising beyond that project.

## Page-level extraction

- The 18-page report identifies Jason Arday as author, Claire Muttock and Jamie Ormes as key contributors, and January 2022 as the report date (cover). It says ThinkHigher commissioned the work as part of its Coventry & Warwickshire partnership and that the project was funded through the Office for Students’ Uni Connect Programme (pp. 4, 7).
- The methodology reports 11 local secondary-school students, aged 15–18, participating in semi-structured interviews over virtual platforms. Five identified as male and six as female. The interview schedule used prompts based on pre-existing race-and-education research; interviews were transcribed and themed using narrative inquiry (p. 8).
- The report says the researchers conducting the interviews were White, and that convenience sampling was used. It expressly warns that the findings may not be generalisable, while presenting them as contextual insight into young Black people’s educational experiences (p. 8).
- Five themes are reported: Eurocentric curriculum; experiences of racism; impacts of racism; hair; and opportunities for change. The report includes subthemes and participant-attributed excerpts on curriculum omission, unfair treatment, microaggressions, stereotyping, exclusion, mental wellbeing, racial gaslighting/confusion, internalisation, hair, staff competence, representation, dialogue, and belonging (pp. 8–17).
- The recommendations call for teaching Black history beyond slavery, curriculum-wide decolonisation, teacher cultural competence, stronger responses to racism, greater staff representation, and spaces of belonging. These are recommendations from a small commissioned qualitative project, not population estimates or independent causal findings (pp. 8–17).
