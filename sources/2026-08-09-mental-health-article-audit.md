# Targeted page-level audit

The preserved 25-page MDPI PDF was read on 9 August 2026. This audit records what the article says it did, what its results consist of, and what it does not establish. It is a scholarly-output record, not a clinical or biographical source about Arday.

## What the paper reports

- **p. 1:** sole author Jason Arday; School of Education, University of Roehampton; received 12 August 2018, accepted 8 October, published 13 October. The abstract says the study used narratives from 32 BME university students and thematic analysis.
- **pp. 10–11 (Methods):** participants were 32 BME people aged 18–34 recruited across 14 UK universities, from undergraduate and postgraduate programmes. The article reports 32 open-ended questionnaires, two unstructured focus-group interviews, and 32 individual semi-structured interviews of approximately 40 minutes each. Recruitment used African and Caribbean university societies, social media, and convenience sampling.
- **p. 10:** the two focus groups each lasted about three hours. The article gives the composition as Asian/Asian British 6, black/black British 14, mixed-heritage 9, and Latin-American 3; 18 women and 14 men; most attended majority-white universities.
- **p. 11:** focus groups took place in April 2017; consent was obtained; sessions and interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim. A topic guide covered views of mental health, cultural differences, access to services, and possible improvements. The paper says thematic analysis was conducted in NVivo, with iterative coding and a second researcher independently reading/coding the material.
- **pp. 11–16:** the reported results are organized around two broad themes: personal/environmental factors and the service-user/healthcare-provider relationship around power and hierarchy. Subthemes include stigma, social networks, gender, cultural identity, language, communication, and power/authority. The paper illustrates these themes with anonymized participant quotations.
- **pp. 16–20 (Discussion):** the paper interprets participants’ accounts as perceived barriers to mental-health access and argues that university and healthcare settings require greater cultural sensitivity, representation, and attention to racialized experiences. It explicitly notes that the sample is a subsection of the broader BME population and may be affected by selection bias.
- **pp. 21–22 (Conclusions):** recommendations include reducing stigma, improving information and access pathways, diversifying staff, developing cultural cognizance, and changing systems that the paper characterizes as institutionally racist. The article records no external funding and declares no conflict of interest.

## Epistemic boundary

This is an empirical qualitative article, not a population-level prevalence study, clinical trial, or independent audit of university mental-health services. Its strongest direct evidence is the article’s account of its own design plus the anonymized participant material as presented by Arday. The PDF does not expose the raw transcripts, coding files, recruitment logs, ethics-committee record, or an independently reproducible participant-level dataset. The article’s broader claims therefore remain bounded by its sample, recruitment method, interpretive framework, and the reported selection-bias limitation.

The methods narrative also says that participants initially expected to disclose more personal mental-illness experiences, but that mental-health professionals advised against eliciting detailed trauma narratives; this is reported on p. 11 and is methodological context, not an independent clinical finding.
