# The Black Curriculum: Black British History in the National Curriculum Report 2021

## What this source is

A report authored by Jason Arday for The Black Curriculum on the place of Black British history in the national curriculum.

## Key facts or claims

- The first-party report page says the report examines the systematic omission of Black British history from the current History National Curriculum.
- The page lists recommendations including diversifying the History National Curriculum, reconceptualising Britishness, diversifying history-teaching workforces, and widening the scope of Black history.
- These are the report’s analysis and recommendations; they should not be presented as an independently verified measurement without checking the report’s underlying methods and sources.

## Page-level extraction

- The 13-page report identifies itself as a January 2021 publication authored by Jason Arday and edited by Lisa Kennedy, Bethany Thompson, Elesha Williams, and Melody Triumph (cover).
- The executive summary says the report examines the omission of Black British history from the History National Curriculum and argues that a curriculum reflecting Britain’s multi-ethnic society would support broader knowledge, teacher capability, social cohesion, and preparation for a diverse society (p. 4).
- The summary recommendations call for: a more multicultural History National Curriculum; a reconception of Britishness that incorporates all histories and stories; a more diverse history-teaching workforce; and teaching Black history as beneficial to Black pupils and British society, including by challenging negative racial tropes (pp. 4–5).
- The report describes its method as content analysis of literature and commentary on Black History in the National Curriculum, including reports, policy documents, blogs, newspaper articles, podcasts, videos/vlogs, and academic papers (p. 7).
- The same methods passage acknowledges limited specific data on patterns of Black-history teaching in primary and secondary schools and difficulty sourcing recent data for parts of the education system (p. 7). The report therefore presents a literature-informed argument and recommendations, not a transparent new school-level prevalence survey.
- The “quick wins” section recommends year-round, cross-disciplinary incorporation of Black History and use of texts by Black authors (pp. 20–21). These are prescriptive proposals within the report, not evidence that the proposals were implemented.
