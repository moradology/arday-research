# The Black Curriculum report: page-level audit

## Object and preservation

The preserved object is a 13-page A4 PDF titled *The Black Curriculum: Black British History in the National Curriculum Report 2021*. Its cover dates it January 2021 and names Jason Arday as author; Lisa Kennedy, Bethany Thompson, Elesha Williams, and Melody Triumph are credited as editors. The local PDF SHA-256 is `e6c4bf7a16191b74ec34deb0ea9262fe2c4d8d0dfd3edfda163dcc33dbb71fa6`. A layout-preserving text derivative is stored alongside it with SHA-256 `24d8d12bb0fd5f42f063ee82f073d2f3fe8fea6d3b2ba7a2967073ea3be5335e`.

The PDF has designed spreads: physical PDF pages and printed page numbers do not always correspond one-to-one. Citations below give both where useful. The PDF, rather than the text derivative, controls exact wording and layout.

## What the report says

- The executive summary (PDF p. 3; printed pp. 4–5) frames the report as an examination of the systematic omission of Black British history from the current History National Curriculum and argues for a curriculum reflecting Britain’s multi-ethnic society.
- The report recommends a more multicultural History National Curriculum, a broader conception of Britishness, a more diverse history-teaching workforce, and teaching Black history as beneficial to Black pupils and British society (PDF p. 3; printed p. 5).
- The introduction and curriculum sections (PDF pp. 4–6; printed pp. 6–11) connect those recommendations to arguments about the 2014 curriculum, “Fundamental British Values,” Eurocentric canons, migration, empire, diaspora, and the risk of reducing Black history to slavery alone.
- The pedagogy sections (PDF pp. 6–8; printed pp. 10–15) argue for teacher preparation, better resources, local/community engagement, and pedagogies capable of addressing a super-diverse classroom. These are proposals and interpretive arguments; the report does not evaluate an implemented intervention.
- The year-round section (PDF p. 9; printed pp. 16–17) argues that Black history should not be confined to Black History Month or October and discusses belonging, representation, colonial/slavery history, and educational inequality.
- The conclusions and “quick wins” (PDF pp. 10–11; printed pp. 19–21) call for a broad, continuous, cross-disciplinary approach, including Black authors in English and Black scientists/mathematicians in science and mathematics, while cautioning against confining Black excellence to sports and entertainment.

## Method and evidence boundary

The report states that it uses content analysis of literature and discourse on Black History in the National Curriculum, drawing on reports, policy documents, blogs, newspaper articles, podcasts, videos/vlogs, and academic papers (PDF p. 4; printed p. 7). It explicitly notes a paucity of specific data on patterns of Black-history teaching in primary and secondary schools, limited data for Black/BME undergraduate History students, and difficulty sourcing recent data for other education tiers.

This makes the report a literature-informed policy argument and recommendation document. The audited PDF does not disclose a newly recruited school sample, survey instrument, administrative dataset, systematic-review search protocol, coded corpus, causal design, or implementation/impact evaluation. Its cited premises require source-specific verification; its recommendations should not be treated as measured outcomes.

## Source-version notes

The biography box (PDF p. 11; printed p. 21) presents dated role and affiliation claims about Arday, including Durham, Ohio State, Nelson Mandela University, Runnymede, the British Sociological Association, the RSA, CLASS, and school governance. Those statements are useful evidence of what this 2021 report said about its author, but they are host-copy/source-version biography, not current-status or independent corroboration.
