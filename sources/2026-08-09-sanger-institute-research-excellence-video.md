# Wellcome Sanger Institute — Research Excellence interview

The Wellcome Sanger Institute’s public YouTube channel published a 9:09 interview titled “Sanger Institute - Research Excellence - Dr Jason Arday, Durham University” on 30 November 2021. The description identifies Arday as an Associate Professor of Sociology at Durham, says he was soon to take up a professorship at Glasgow, and summarizes his research as intersecting education, social mobility, mental health, and race. It also names his then-described Runnymede Trust trusteeship and authorship of the 2019 *Leading Routes* report *The Broken Pipeline*.

The video is a direct audiovisual source for Arday’s own answers, but the statements below remain self-report. The recovered English captions are YouTube automatic captions and should be checked against the picture and sound before use as verbatim quotations.

## Subject-centred passages

- **00:39–01:08:** Arday says he completed his PhD in two and a half years while working full-time. He says he had a brain tumour at the time, had it removed two weeks before his viva, and had a stroke immediately afterwards. He describes passing the viva as significant because he did not know how far he had got across the line.
- **01:30–03:10:** He says his job at the time depended on obtaining the PhD, that he had just become a father for the second time, and that his 2015 PhD and 2019 Durham appointment were defining points. He describes the Durham appointment as a moment when he felt he might be realizing his potential.
- **03:33–05:29:** Asked about his research, he describes work on Black and ethnic-minority experiences, working-class backgrounds, intersectional inequality, psychological well-being, racial trauma, looked-after children navigating education, and institutional systems. He says he advised the NHS on training for therapists and counsellors and says he qualified as a counsellor roughly two and a half years before the interview.
- **06:27–08:42:** Discussing the Black Curriculum report, he argues that teaching Black and minority histories matters for Black children and for all children. He frames education as preparing people to participate in society and describes the absence of that history as a form of historical amnesia that leaves students less prepared for multi-ethnic and diverse populations.
