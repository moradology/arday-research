# *Revista História Hoje*: interview with Jason Arday

Amilcar Araujo Pereira interviewed Jason Arday at the University of Cambridge on 28 November 2023. The interview was published on 11 December 2024 in *Revista História Hoje*, volume 13, issue 29, as “Um professor negro em Cambridge: Entrevista com Jason Arday” / “A Black Professor in Cambridge.” The article page identifies it as an interview, gives DOI `10.20949/rhhj.v13i29.1268`, and licenses the work under CC BY 4.0.

## What the interview adds

The article is not merely a publisher biography. In the interview, Arday speaks in the first person about his family background, childhood observations, charity motivation, Sandro’s influence, and intellectual formation. The following summaries are translated from the published Portuguese version and retain the interview’s source status; they are not presented as verbatim English quotations.

### Family, poverty, and childhood observation

On printed pp. 252–253, Arday says that his parents were Ghanaian and South African and that the family was very poor. He recalls his parents regularly welcoming people who had little money, cooking for them, feeding them, and sending leftovers home. He says his mother took him as a child to homeless shelters so he could see how other people lived, and connects these experiences to his later emphasis on compassion and giving.

He also says that people felt sorry for him because he could not speak, but that the absence of speech allowed him to observe and listen. In the same passage he says he was deaf in one ear while still able to hear. These are first-person recollections in a translated, edited interview—not independent medical or contemporaneous family records.

### The shelter encounter and charity narrative

On printed pp. 253–254, Arday recounts a Christmas visit to a homeless shelter at age 18, where he says he met a formerly respected professor who had lost his family, property, and livelihood after a series of events. Arday presents this encounter as a formative conversation about judging people and listening to their circumstances. He says it led him to tell his mother that he would raise as much money as possible for charity and dedicate his life to helping people.

The same account says he set a goal of raising £1 million before age 40, reached that goal by age 30, and had raised £5 million by the time of the interview. These are self-reported totals and an anecdotal account; the article does not provide a charity audit, named shelter record, or documentary corroboration for the encounter or the cumulative figure.

### Sandro and intellectual formation

The interview returns to Sandro’s “will versus ability” formulation, presenting Sandro as telling Arday that he lacked ability but possessed an unmeasurable determination. Later, on printed p. 262, Arday says his mother was a Black feminist and that his intellectual orientation was shaped by Paulo Freire, bell hooks, Shirley Anne Tate, Patricia Hill Collins, and Kimberlé Crenshaw, among others. The wording is a first-person account of influence and identity formation, not an independent account of Sandro’s or his mother’s biography.

## Provenance limits

The article’s introductory biography repeats familiar claims about diagnosis, delayed speech and literacy, education, Cambridge, and charity. Those repetitions are not counted as independent corroboration. The value of this source is the dated interview context and the additional first-person material about family practices, poverty, the shelter encounter, deafness in one ear, charity goals, and intellectual influences.

Because the published text is translated and edited, English-language wording should not be reconstructed as a verbatim quotation without consulting the original recording or an English transcript. The PDF is a lawful public CC BY manifestation and is locally preserved; no interview audio was exposed by the journal page.
