# Guardian black-history curriculum commentary — page audit

Canonical page: <https://www.theguardian.com/commentisfree/2020/jun/13/equal-britain-teach-black-history-empire>

The Guardian page identifies Jason Arday as author, dates publication to 13 June 2020, and reports a last modification on 15 June 2020. The page-end biography describes him at that point as an assistant professor of sociology at Durham University. This is an authored opinion/commentary page, not a peer-reviewed study or an independent audit of curriculum outcomes.

## Education and historical knowledge

Arday presents education as a means of disarming ignorance and bigotry. He invokes the 1999 Macpherson report and the 2020 Windrush Lessons Learned review as policy sources that recommended Black history in school syllabuses, then argues that little meaningful progress had occurred. The article’s account is a cited policy synthesis and public argument; the underlying recommendations and the “no significant advancement” assessment were not independently reanalysed here.

## Empire, Black contributions, and curriculum

The article argues that British history education is shaped by “deliberate amnesia”: atrocities and profits connected to empire, and Black contributions to Britain, are under-taught, while Second World War narratives centre white British experience. Arday calls for the curriculum to decentre whiteness and a Eurocentric canon and to include empire and migrant contributions. These are his analysis and recommendations, not findings from a dataset supplied by the page.

## Intersectionality and attainment

Arday uses the history of Black feminist organiser Olive Morris to illustrate intersectionality and misogynoir. He also argues that the absence of a diverse curriculum harms Black students’ attainment and prevents students from learning their history. The page does not provide a causal design, outcome dataset, or independent evaluation for those claims; preserve them as attributed public positions.

## Policy and implementation

The commentary highlights the Black Curriculum organisation, founded by Lavinya Stennett in January 2019, and describes its work as reimagining education through Black British history, arts, and teacher training. Arday calls for central government support, professional support for teachers, and a broader national narrative. These are recommendations and descriptions of an organisation, not evidence that the proposed changes were implemented or produced specified effects.
