Jason Arday on his remarkable journey from non-verbal child to Cambridge professor

“All of this work I do is a privilege. But my success is reflective of nothing apart from how unequal a system can be and how one individual managed to navigate it.”
Author InterviewsJun 12, 2026by Caroline Sanderson

The writer and academic’s debut memoir tells his story, from non-verbal child written off by experts to the youngest Black professor in Cambridge University’s history.

Caroline Sanderson

Caroline Sanderson worked as a Waterstones bookseller, and as a book publicist before becoming a freelance writer in 1997. The Bookseller’s

...more

When Jason Arday’s mother – known to all as Giff – was pregnant with her second son, she read a lot of Greek mythology. Her favourite story was Jason and the Argonauts; so when he was born, she gave him that hero’s name. She was not to know then that, just like his namesake, her Jason would also face many trials, obstacles and tests of courage.

Born in 1985 and raised in Clapham, south London, Arday was diagnosed with a form of autism spectrum disorder at the age of three and spent much of his non-verbal childhood navigating an education system that struggled to cater for his needs. Arday did not speak until he was 11 and was unable to read or write until he was 18. Therapists and career advisers predicted he would spend his adult life in assisted living and require lifelong support. But Giff refused to accept these limits. In time, her son went to college, trained as a teacher, and then launched his academic career. At 27, while studying for his PhD, he wrote a set of personal goals on a bedroom wall at his parents’ home. One of them read: “One day I will work at Oxford or Cambridge.”

In 2023, at the age of 37, Arday became the youngest Black professor ever appointed in the long and illustrious history of Cambridge University. Professor of sociology of education, he is a respected scholar of education, race, inequality and intersectionality. Thanks to his high profile he is widely recognised wherever he goes as ‘the Cambridge guy’, including at the recent football Premier League victory parade he attended with his two “Arsenal nut” brothers.

A Cambridge professorship would be a stellar achievement for anyone. But as his unforgettable memoir, Great and Unfortunate Things, shows, for Arday it was like laying hands on the Golden Fleece. That oft-used word ‘inspirational’ feels wholly inadequate to describe his incredible story of grit and resilience.

To this day, everything I do is scrutinised and challenged in a way that almost never happens to my white colleagues.

When we meet at his publisher Simon & Schuster’s offices, Arday – a warm, softly spoken man with a quiet dignity – tells me that within a week of his historic Cambridge appointment that made headlines around the world, he had received more than 150 emails from literary agents in the UK and US. One stood out: that from Jennifer Gates at Aevitas Creative Management in New York. “Her email was really powerful and made me want to talk to her because she was clearly a very special individual,” Arday tells me. Over the next 15 months, they crafted a proposal together and his memoir was acquired by Simon & Schuster on pre-empt on both sides of the Atlantic.

So far, so exciting. But Arday characterises the two-year process of writing his memoir with co-writer Eve Claxton as an extremely tough one, and is full of praise for how the Simon & Schuster team, including his UK editor Kris Doyle, supported and nurtured him through it. “I don’t think many race scholars would describe their work as fun right now given the dominant rhetoric of these turbulent times. When you’re up against the zeitgeist,you feel highly exposed and vulnerable,” he tells me.

In Great and Unfortunate Things, Arday writes that “the suggestion that I somehow wasn’t worthy of my success would be levied against me time and again… To this day, everything I do is scrutinised and challenged in a way that almost never happens to my white colleagues”.

Writing his story in the face of such trials, Arday kept thinking of another myth, that of Icarus. “I always wanted to be a person who flew close to the sun. Giff would say to me: ‘A lot of people dream of flying close to the sun but fear paralyses them. The fear is understandable, but you have to do this, Jason.’ But sometimes I wonder: did I fly too close?”

There are other heroic figures in Arday’s story. They include his hard-grafting, working-class Ghanaian immigrant parents – especially Giff, who bestowed on him an “unshakeable sense of self”. His first girlfriend and later wife Debs also staunchly believed in his abilities. And after Arday left secondary school with two GCSEs to his name, still barely able to read or write, a remarkable college course leader named Sandro took a chance on him and allowed him on to a BTEC course in sport despite his lack of qualifications. “Sandro said to me: ‘These are the things you’re rubbish at.’ And he listed about 100. Then he said: ‘The one thing you’ve got is that you seem like someone who has an indomitable will. Skill can be measured but will is immeasurable. So we can harness that will into something.’ ”

Great and Unfortunate Things is also a rounded portrait of a man with a talent for football, snooker and sewing, and a penchant for Savile Row tailoring, whose inspirations include sport, soap operas, music and movies (notably those starring Sylvester Stallone).

It is a spiritual tale too of altruism, of religious faith, and even – as befits a man named after a Greek hero – a hint of prophecy. When Arday was born, the South Asian midwife who delivered him studied his long fingers and told Giff: “Where I come from, babies with long fingers are special. This boy is destined for great things.” That midwife prophesied correctly. But as readers will discover, the great things he achieved did not come without cost, and the years of studying and striving, while masking his neurodivergence in the face of intolerance, eventually took a toll.

Most importantly, Great and Unfortunate Things is a book about how it should not be so hard for a gifted, neurodivergent, working-class child of colour to shine. Making that less difficult is now Arday’s life mission.

It is not just his trailblazing academic achievements that make Arday special. It is also his courage, his genuine humility and his clarion sense of calling, with less than 1% of UK rofessorships currently held by Black academics. “All of this work I do is a privilege,” he says. “But my success is reflective of nothing apart from how unequal a system can be and how one individual managed to navigate it. One individual navigating a system does not cultivate the collective change that we all need.”

Since his professorship hit the headlines, Arday has received hundreds of thousands of letters and emails. “It’s amazing how many people view me as a great underdog story. But if all you take from my story is that I ended up a Cambridge professor, you’re missing the point.”
