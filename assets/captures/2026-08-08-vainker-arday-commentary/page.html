The Wreckage

Leave Jason Arday alone - this is a scandal of academic educational leadership

Stephen Vainker
Jul 27, 2026

Jason Arday absolutely doesn’t deserve all the nasty criticism. He was a young scholar trying to make his way, and like lots of us but more so, made huge mistakes in search of the prize of a successful academic career. A lot of the criticism - most of the criticism - has been racially charged, both directed towards Arday as a Black man and towards his work in critical race theory. It goes without saying that this type of criticism of Arday should be rejected - not merely because it’s unpleasant, but because it obscures, somewhat, the actual significance of the Arday case.

The real significance of the Arday case is that it serves as a further demonstration that the field of educational academia has lost a sense of its primary, substantive purpose: to generate and share knowledge that will improve education. But academia is now extremely tightly managed, and is judged against various more-or-less politicised metrics at every level from the institution to the individual academic. This managerialism, as we all know by now, has perverse consequences. Not only is research judged against spurious measures of quality and impact that may well have very little to do with the actual impact on policy and practice, but academia is judged against broader social metrics, including those related to diversity and inclusion, that may very well serve to distract from or supersede academic issues.

In the case of educational academia, none of these complaints are original, but I would suggest that this has resulted in:

  • too much of the work becoming detached from the realities and requirements of classroom practice; where there are implications, they are generally facile;

  • too much of the work being derivative and unoriginal, narrowly focused on relatively arcane corners of theory (including but certainly not limited to critical race theory);

  • secondary characteristics, including but not limited to identity markers, playing a central role in hiring and promotion, over and above ability and quality.

But far worse than this, the leaders in the field do not seem to be interested in putting the field back on track - they are more concerned with following what is strategically useful to maintaining their status in the fantastical world of the university.

Hattie and BERA

To illustrate the significance of Arday’s case, I want to draw on some of the projects I’ve been interested in - John Hattie and the British Educational Research Association (BERA). Hattie’s case shows that the problem of not properly investigating integrity issues is not unique to Cambridge, or Arday, or primarily a question of race. After I demonstrated clear and incontrovertible evidence of extensive plagiarism by Hattie in some of the most important works of 21st century educational scholarship, the University of Melbourne performed the most laughably purposefully blind investigation imaginable. Hattie and Arday are both stars valuable to the reputation of the university, so they were protected.

The comparison between their scholarship is also instructive. Hattie’s quantitative research was finely tuned to chime with the aims of educational policymakers - to make education more efficient through the adoption of business practices. Although very different, Arday’s qualitative work in critical race theory was equally attuned to political currents of the day, in terms of how institutions are now legitimating themselves through EDI work. So again, the important aspect of Arday’s work is not related to race, rather political usefulness for the institution.

The case of BERA, which also has similarities to the Arday case, is equally instructive. I have shown that over the last few years, the four BERA-affiliated journals have published obviously poor quality AI-written work including over 20 articles with fake references. This has happened because, having set targets to increase the diversity of authorship in the journals, they have apparently relaxed the rigour of peer review for historically under-represented identities. In the face of evidence of flawed research, they have doubled down, refusing to take action and saying that my approach was ‘ethically problematic’. The BERA case should be understood in part as the reaction of a body of academics who have been sidelined since Gove from performing their primary role - informing educational policymaking and practice. In response, they have retreated to legitimating themselves through managerialist targets related to identity.

The case of Cremin

So, the case of Arday should be understood in the following terms. A mistake, but not the biggest mistake, was hiring him because of his identity rather than his scholarship. The really telling mistake though, was how the university handled the revelation, soon after he was appointed, that his scholarship was illegitimate. This is what Professor Hilary Cremin, the Head of the Education Faculty, wrote to the internal complainant about Arday’s plagiarism:

[W]e remain immensely proud of Prof Arday’s achievements and scholarship, and...he has our full support.

…During our meeting Prof Arday spoke movingly about his ongoing experiences of direct and indirect racism, and the conditions that he was living in whilst the 2018 article was being prepared (in 2016). At this time he was early career and living away from home whilst dealing with significant health issues.

It is here that an educational academic leader - seemingly without a great deal of reflection - insisted the university was ‘immensely proud’ of shoddy scholarship and explained away the plagiarism by saying a 31 year old was ‘living away from home’. In other words, she turned her back on finding out the truth about the value of Arday’s scholarship and insisted, tacitly but nevertheless with absolute institutional force, that he was beyond criticism.

Why is this moment telling? Because it reveals, with absolute clarity, that educational academic leaders are willing, without hesitation, to sideline academic integrity in order to protect figures who legitimate them in our highly politicised managerialist system. As demonstrated by Melbourne and BERA, they are totally invested in their reputations based on secondary issues such as EDI at the expense of their core role.

Conclusion

So, race does matter in the Arday affair - which should really be the Cremin affair - but not in a direct, straightforward way. Jason Arday was not protected by the university because he’s Black - if he was a white superstar researcher doing bad quantitative research he would have been similarly sheltered from scrutiny. Rather, his race was an opportunity for leaders such as Cremin to legitimate themselves without doing the difficult work of making educational academia relevant and actually useful again.

The problem is that universities, and educational departments in particular, are existing in a fabricated world, where they are judged on measures other than their basic core purpose. Educational academic leaders need the vision to discern the substantive purpose of educational research from the fake metrics, but it is clear they are complacent enough to be happy to take the easy wins related to EDI and various impact measures, even if this means ignoring and sabotaging their true purpose. The tragedy is that the current generation of leaders are currently grooming the next generation in their image; it is hard to see any future for educational academia beyond increasing obscurity.
