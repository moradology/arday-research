Just Look at the Data

Published by 21percent.org on 29 July 202629 July 2026

It’s best to look at the data directly and form your own conclusions from it (as @TheResearcher has been saying).

Just look at the damn data.

Run some tests or do some simple visual checks. That way, your perspective is rooted in actual evidence, rather than filtered through second-hand opinions or culture-war narratives.

The two PhD theses are still online: Jason Arday’s here, Paula Zwozdiak-Myers downloadable here if you want to check primary sources. The data are available here on Professor David Harris’ website. These are the materials that Prof Harris, Jack Grove (Times Higher Education) and others submitted to Cambridge and Liverpool John Moores University in September 2025. .

Dr Rhys Morgan, Head of Policy, Governance and Integrity at University of Cambridge, then asked Professor Manuel Eisner, Director of the Institute of Criminology, to conduct an inquiry and write a report.

This report must now be provided, as well as any corresponding report written by Liverpool John Moores University. This is necessary as the Good Law Project have circulated a solidarity letter, claiming

Entirely false allegations of plagiarism were made against Professor Arday and referred to two universities. Investigations have found no evidence whatsoever of wrongdoing.

The letter has been signed by three Heads of House: Lord Simon Woolley, (Principal of Homerton College), Professor Dame Ijeoma Uchegbu (President of Wolfson College) and Sonia Alleyne (Master of Jesus College). It has also been signed by two Cambridge academics, Professor Simon Baron-Cohen and Professor Ricardo Sabates Aysa.

Cambridge’s investigation did not clear Prof Arday of plagiarism, but rather ruled the investigation out of scope.

Our advice is anyone considering signing the letter should first look at the data.

For us, this is not primarily about Prof Jason Arday as an individual. He is not the only case in which individuals appear to have been protected and rewarded within networks at Cambridge University for behaviour that amounts to dishonesty and misconduct. Rather, he is simply the case that has come to light — perhaps for reasons that are not entirely reassuring.

Our primary concern is the complete failure of process. There should be a formal investigation into how these matters have been handled by the Research Policy, Integrity and Governance division. The current situation raises serious questions about both the integrity of that process and the clarity of its remit, with significant implications for the University’s reputation and its duty of care.

We are also aware of other cases in which the Research Policy, Integrity and Governance division appears to have handled matters inadequately, reinforcing the need for a thorough and transparent review.

It is often Early Career Researchers, such as Dr Magdalen Connelly, who have the courage to raise concerns about potential research misconduct by senior academics. These individuals are typically in the most vulnerable position, with limited institutional power and significant personal and professional risks attached to speaking out.

We further believe that these achievements should render close examination of his academic work unnecessary, if not actively improper.

We regard these accomplishments as relevant not merely to his character, but also to the entirely separate question of whether conventional citation practices should apply to him. Good works, in our view, should function as a transferable stock of academic attribution credits.

The discussion of Professor Arday, and more recently of Misan Harriman, risks creating a chilling effect. Successful public figures may begin to fear that journalists will inspect their work, compare it with earlier sources, and publish what they find. Such scrutiny could discourage people from attaining positions of leadership—or, at the very least, from borrowing material quite so confidently.

We therefore stand in unwavering solidarity not only with Professor Arday, but with the broader principle that plagiarism should be assessed primarily by reference to the identity, achievements and political sympathies of the person accused.

We call on universities to defend academic freedom by discouraging robust examination of scholarship. They must confront racism wherever it manifests itself, while also recognizing that allegations of inadequate attribution can be neutralized by describing those who raise them as politically objectionable.

There can be no place in higher education for campaigns that weaponize textual comparison to damage reputations and careers. Universities must ensure that excellence is protected and that every scholar is judged not by such narrow technicalities as originality, quotation marks and citations, but by whether sufficiently distinguished signatories are willing to say that the matter is closed.
