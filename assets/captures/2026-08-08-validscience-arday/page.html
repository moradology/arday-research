Background

Arday publishes junk studies where he claims to have interviewed a few minority students, then purportedly quotes from those interviews. There’s no further analysis, not even systematic text analysis, just some painfully written discussion of the alleged quotes. Apparently, sociologists don’t provide the full quotes when they use this method (e.g. in an Appendix), making the studies pointless since it’s too easy for biased researchers to selectively quote people and exclude some participants entirely.

He frames his “studies” around the new leftist race ideology — Critical Race Theory (CRT) — which sorts humans into racial groups, defines them by their race and other group “identities”, and asserts that minorities are surrounded and grievously harmed by “systemic”, “structural”, or “institutional” racism, that we’re “marginalized” and “oppressed”, and that “systems” must be “decolonized”, including academia, in order to end this alleged state of marginalization and oppression.

To the extent these claims can be falsified, they’re false. There’s no systemic racism against blacks and browns in 2026, nor has there been for many decades, and the reality is in fact the opposite — it’s an advantage to be a minority in many contexts, both in the UK and the US. For example, it’s easier to gain admittance at every academic stage (elite high schools, college, graduate programs, medical schools, law schools, etc.) and easier to get a faculty job. That is, minorities can secure admission with lower test scores and grades, and secure job offers with fewer and lower quality publications, than white applicants1. It’s not minorities who suffer discrimination — it’s white males and especially non-leftists of all colors.

Reality has an anti-leftist bias and since sociology is dominated by radical leftists the field is not a reality-congruent domain. Moreover, ideological constructs like “marginalized” and “oppressed” applied to the 21st-century West mark a novel psychology and optional cosmology rather than testable, coherent claims about the world. You won’t find constructs of that nature in other ideologies and schools (conservative, libertarian, etc.)

Further, it’s invalid for scholars to assert an ideology as descriptive reality. That is, they don’t understand the difference between saying “service users can be acutely aware of the provider’s framed credentials” and “service users can be acutely aware of the centrality of whiteness” (Arday) — one refers to descriptive referents (a framed degree on a wall) while the other embeds a hostile abstract construct drawn from a particular, non-universal political ideology, a construct that doesn’t refer to something consensually real and which is not scientifically valid or meaningful. Plagiarized Data

Retired academic Dave Harris discovered the first instance of data plagiarism. Take a look at this passage:

The need for a positive interpersonal connection between service user and healthcare provider where the consultation was “a dialogue” (Male D. FG1) was seen as an important factor in engaging with mental health services.

It’s from Memon et al. (2016). They interviewed 26 minorities in Southeast England about access to mental health services.

In 2018, Arday published a study titled Understanding Mental Health: What Are the Issues for Black and Ethnic Minority Students at University?, where he claims to have interviewed 32 minority students about mental health issues at university. Harris found this passage:

‘Positive, interpersonal dialogue between service users and healthcare providers’ (Female, Black, UG, 27) where the consultation was ‘a reciprocal dialogue’ (Male, Asian, PG, 25). This was considered to be an important factor in facilitating better ethnic minority engagement with mental health services at universities and wider society.

Note that the original Memon et al. passage is largely unquoted — it’s the writing of the authors, not the quoted words of a participant. Arday converted Memon’s body text into two participants’ quotes, with the “positive, interpersonal” and “between service users and healthcare providers” bits, and copied the “consultation was a (reciprocal) dialogue” and the “was seen/considered to be an important factor in engaging with mental health services” bits.

He fabricated the participants’ quotes, meaning there may not have been any participants — or any study — or perhaps they didn’t say what he wanted them to say.

I found many more examples where Arday stole participant quotes from Memon et al. These examples are all drawn from his originally published paper (there have been several “corrections” since).

Data Plagiarism #2: Pen strokes and the dose makes the poison

Here’s Memon:

There was concern that clinicians had “the power simply with the stroke of a pen to decide whether you are going to be sectioned or whether you are going to be sent away for some counselling. Or […] administered some really, really high doses of whatever kind of medicine” (Male D. FG1).

Here’s Arday:

There was concern that, in some cases, clinicians could potentially abuse their power. ‘Let’s face it . . . with the stroke of a pen you could be sectioned or sent away for some counseling. Or … have administered some ridiculous dose of whatever kind of medicine’ (Male, Black, UG, 13).

This is near verbatim theft, and he even copied the ellipses after Or...

Data Plagiarism #3: West Indian particulars

Memon:

“In the West Indian community […] people do not trust psychiatrists particularly […] in terms of where my family and friends would turn...” (Male D. FG1).

Arday:

‘Within African and West Indian communities . . . people do not trust psychiatrists in particular.’ (Female, Black, UG, 13).

Arday merely changed particularly to in particular, and he again copied the ellipses…

Data Plagiarism #4: Really, really

Memon:

“the mental health service does not always understand. That we are really, really isolated” (Female I. FG1). Social isolation was also relevant for people who lived alone, or whose family structure had broken down…

Arday:

‘The mental health services provided at universities did not always understand all of the racialized nuances involved’ (Male, Mixed-Heritage, PG, 1). Further, one participant added that we are really, really isolated in this space as black people’ (Female, Latin-American, UG, 7).

Social isolation external from university environments was also relevant for participants who lived alone or whose family structure was fractured for varying reasons.

Arday copied the “mental health services does not always understand” and the “that we are really, really isolated” bits (he’s missing the opening quote there). He even copied Memon’s sentence after the quote, breaking it off into a new paragraph and making minor changes.

And again, Arday industriously converts one of Memon’s participants into two fictional participants of his own.

Strangely, Arday also copied the section headings from Memon (see my Appendix below). His paper is a mirror of Memon et al.

Leftists will counter that the outcome disparities are proof that “systemic racism” exists and causes the disparities. That’s not how proof works. We don’t know the cause of an outcome simply by observing the outcome — that’s a fallacy known as Affirming the Consequent. And for outcomes like average income, we already know the predictors: choice of career/major, hard work, IQ, out-of-wedlock birth rates, single-parent households, criminality, etc. The first, obvious task for someone arguing for systemic racism causing minorities to have lower average incomes than whites (or Asians, Jews, or Lebanese) would be to show that incomes are lower even after controlling for all relevant variables. It could easily just be culture, and that culture doesn’t have to be genetically destined.
