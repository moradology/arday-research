Cambridge to review process for hiring senior academics after Jason Arday resignation

[Professor Jason Arday]Image source, ITV / Shutterstock

ByAleks Phillips

- Published

  7 August 2026

The University of Cambridge will review its process for appointing
senior academics following the resignation of Jason Arday in the midst
of a plagiarism row.

The institution said findings from an investigation into the education
professor's academic qualifications and appointments would "feed into" a
new review on the appointment process for senior academic roles.

The Jesus College fellow has also faced accusations of fabricating or
exaggerating elements of his CV. He has defended himself and his work,
with attention now turning to Cambridge's handling of concerns and his
upcoming memoir.

News of the review comes after a group of academics called for an
independent probe into his appointment.

Priyamvada Gopal, professor of postcolonial studies at Cambridge and
among those calling for an independent inquiry, said the university was
facing "massive reputational damage" over the affair.

"This is reputational damage that has upset colleagues across
disciplines, across the political spectrum," she told BBC Radio 4's
Today programme on Friday.

"I've been quite struck by the number of people who are very upset by
what has happened. So I don't think it is an option for Cambridge to
just sweep this under the carpet and not take any action at all."

Gopal said the university should not be "marking its own homework",
adding that the group of academics of which she is a part had suggested
a mix of senior academics at Cambridge and elsewhere to conduct an
investigation.

Cambridge said on Friday that it was "fully aware of the public
commentary and concerns" and would "continue to investigate the
circumstances around Jason Arday's appointment and tenure". A
spokesperson declined to comment further.

It previously announced an investigation into "new information" about
Arday's academic qualifications and appointments on Wednesday, having
already said it was looking into allegations of plagiarism.

Jesus College welcomed the appointments review, saying Arday's role as
fellow there had been "contingent on his university position".

"We rely on the university to do checks on academic credentials for its
appointments," a spokeswoman added.

The University of Glasgow, where Arday worked for just over a year
before moving to Cambridge, has commissioned its own review of his
appointment.

"Jason Arday was employed as Professor of Sociology of Education at the
University of Glasgow, from January 2022 to March 2023," a statement
said.

"The Principal has commissioned a review of the appointment with the aim
of understanding all the facts and learning lessons for the future."

Accusations of plagiarism in Arday's work were first voiced by Nathan
Cofnas - a US researcher whose role at a Cambridge college was
terminated after backlash against his view that under a true meritocracy
black people would "disappear from almost all high-profile positions
outside of sports and entertainment".

He highlighted that plagiarism detection software had found several
tracts of Arday's PhD dissertation to be copied verbatim or lightly
rephrased from prior academic works by other authors.

Liverpool John Moores University (LJMU), which awarded Arday's PhD, has
said it investigated the allegations of plagiarism last year and
dismissed them, saying they were likely the result of "honest and
reasonable error".

It told the BBC on Friday that its position remains unchanged in light
of recent events and that his PhD "still stands".

Cofnas also noted that a retired professor had previously raised
concerns about Arday's subsequent academic articles, which led to two
journals issuing correction notes, and raised questions about some of
his claimed achievements, including extensive marathon running and
raising millions for charity.

Several universities at which Arday claimed on his CV to have had
academic positions have since denied him having those roles.

The controversy has attracted a range of opinions, including within
academia, about whether he had been scrutinised unfairly because of his
race.

Arday has admitted errors in his work but denied plagiarism, saying his
autism and a lack of adequate supervision early in his academic career
may have contributed to those mistakes. He also denied lying about his
achievements.

He said his decision to step down should not "be mistaken for an
acceptance of the narratives that have surrounded me", which he has
previously said were "racially motivated".

Gopal said universities were "being targeted in particular by a lazy and
false division between academic excellence and diversity and equality on
campus", which was "really part of culture wars rather than any part of
university life".

She and her colleagues found it "unusual" that Arday had been appointed
to a "very senior, very prestigious" post early in his career - and that
he had been put forward in the media with "frankly a bit of a target on
his back".

Gopal also said any investigation should review "whether the university
was involved in any way or any level with efforts to deter journalists
from reporting on the allegations".

This came after it emerged the Metropolitan Police investigated Times
Higher Education (THE) reporter Jack Grove over a complaint of
harassment after he contacted Arday concerning allegations of plagiarism
last year.

The Telegraph also reported that Arday hired law firm Carter-Ruck to
send a letter to THE which implied that the "apparent attack" on Arday
might be "racially motivated".

The Met closed the case and Commissioner Sir Mark Rowley apologised for
the investigation, telling LBC on Thursday that the complaint should
have been "screened out earlier on as not requiring investigation".

Great and unfortunate things

Among the claims by Arday that have been viewed as outlandish is one,
made in an interview with the Guardian, that he was paid "1.4 million"
for his memoir, Great and Unfortunate Things, due to be released next
week. He did not specify the currency.

While not inconceivable, publishing insiders cast doubt on a figure such
as Arday being able to attract such a large advance payment.

Publisher Simon & Schuster has said it plans to press ahead with the
publication despite the controversy surrounding Arday.

But the Atlantic reported on Thursday that there were discrepancies
between an advance copy of the memoir and the proposal for the book from
2024 it had obtained.

Among the differences, the magazine said the proposal gave his suicide
attempt as happening before the Covid pandemic, whereas in the memoir it
occurred during, and that the proposal detailed Arday being hit by a car
as a teenager before "miraculously" reawakening, an episode omitted from
the finished book.

It comes after the Telegraph reported that Arday had claimed to have
already published a book entitled Being Young, Black and Male:
Challenging the Dominant Discourse.

Palgrave Macmillan, which was attributed to its publication, told the
newspaper it had accepted a proposal for the book but did not publish
it.

The BBC has contacted Simon & Schuster and the Good Law Project, which
has publicly represented Arday during the plagiarism row, for comment.
