We See Things They’ll Never See by Chantelle Jessica Lewis and Jason Arday

Reviewed by Alexa MacDermot
9th September 2025
From the September 2025 Magazine
Chantelle Jessica Lewis and Jason Arday
We See Things They’ll Never See: Love, Hope, and Neurodiversity
Princeton University Press,  2025
https://doi.org/10.1080/01419870.2025.2501132

In We See Things They’ll Never See: Love, Hope, and Neurodiversity, Chantelle Jessica Lewis and Jason Arday address the largely unquestioned discrimination against divergent neurotypes, such as Autism and ADHD. Through the lenses of sociological theory, music, love and advocacy, they describe a British neurodivergent past, present and potential future. They present a manifesto for change and inclusion with an emphasis that we don’t have to wait for a utopian future – a better life for us all is possible today.

Lewis and Arday bring together neurodivergent scholarship, Black experience and scrutiny of socio-economic disparities in British society to explore what life could be like if different neurotypes were universally accepted. Using music as a time machine, the authors transport the reader to different stages of their lives – from their personal experiences as Black neurodivergent students in the British education system, to their present academic careers. The lyrics from songs by artists such as Tina Turner and Oasis, will resonate with anyone who understands the minoritised experiences of race, neurodivergence and discrimination due to being outside the “norm”.

Framing sociological concepts around familiar songs (those chosen will be particularly recognisable to those of us who were teenagers in the 1990s) has a decolonising effect, and using creative methods such as autoethnography in an academic text legitimises the researchers’ personal experiences. This approach has its roots in Black feminism, and Lewis and Arday acknowledge they stand on the shoulders of thinkers including Patricia Hill Collins and Audre Lorde. Using music to assist an academic endeavour is a striking feature that highlights the opportunities and joys of neurodivergent thinking.

The authors explore race and class in a capitalist society to reveal how minoritised people bear a heavier burden than the majority population and describe the points at which neurodivergent people can find themselves excluded by this system of oppression. Equality isn’t possible within the fortified structures of institutions and education while racism and classism exist. Through compassion for difference, intersectionality can bring people together instead of separating them along divides of race, gender, disability and so on.

Love is a tool that can facilitate the necessary change of perspective that Lewis and Arday advocate for. It assists our ability to embrace differences that might usually be concealed by structural systems that remain unchallenged. As Black scholars teaching in universities, the authors acknowledge their privileged positions within the structure of education while still demanding change from within. Their position as Black academics demanding change is radical, their calls for equality and equity are feminist, and the way they draw attention to a racist, classist and ableist past that has shaped present-day attitudes is decolonial.

We should acknowledge how vital and important it is for two scholars to publish their cultural experiences of being both neurodivergent and Black in Britain. This visibility is both remarkable and crucial: it challenges racist assumptions and abolishes any notion that minoritised neurodivergent people can’t reach the highest academic achievements.

In the afterword, the authors address historical prejudice against Black neurodivergent people. They detail the “educationally subnormal” (ESN) schools that existed throughout the UK in the 1960s and 1970s. Targeted children – the majority of whom were Black and minority ethnic (BME) – were sent to these schools in a racially oriented effort to subdue and diminish non-White migrant people. These experiences scarred a generation of BME people, many of whom later came to identify as neurodivergent. Lewis and Arday also identify other areas of education that continue to disproportionately isolate their neurodivergent “siblings”.

A key point is that “neurotypical hegemony”, the concept that a “normal” neurotype can only be one without divergences such as Autism, ADHD, dyslexia and others, damages us all, not just those it excludes. The “neurodiversity paradigm”, as described by the scholar Nick Walker and neurodivergent activist Kassiane Asasumasu, states that all neurotypes are valid and part of humanity and that a society which cannot accept this creates a restrictive rigidity around what it means to be human.

Constructing barriers around difference – neurotype, race, ethnicity, gender and more – means experiential nuance is framed as a negative instead of celebrated as part of the richness of humanity. Lewis and Arday describe this blinkered view as “sharpness” and argue that it causes great pain to people outside the “normal” majority. Relaxing this rigidity with love and acceptance would improve the lives of everyone outside of the hegemonic “normal”.

The authors offer ideas for action in the form of an 11-point map to freedom from ableism, racism and capitalism. I recognise the urgency in providing an actionable list for improvement following the outline of a problem, which I regard as a neurodivergent characteristic. Lewis and Arday’s call to action is a visceral demand for change, particularly in education, that we can all implement in our daily lives. This is why we need more representation of neurodivergent scholars in research such as this: visibility leads to inclusion and compassion.

Lewis and Arday’s storytelling complements and enriches the complex theoretical analysis of Black neurodivergent experience without sensationalising minoritised people’s lives. This balance is not easy, and the shared stories leave you wanting to know more, highlighting the need for more autoethnography in academic texts.

As an autistic White neurodivergence researcher, I want to contribute my own song to summarise my experience of We See Things They’ll Never See: Love, Hope, and Neurodiversity. The teenage song that held meaning for me was Des’ree’s 1994 hit You Gotta Be, in which she sings “Listen as your day unfolds, challenge what the future holds, try and keep your head up to the sky.” Today, neurodivergent researchers with multiple intersecting identities are being included in neurodivergence research, and with scholars such as Chantelle Jessica Lewis and Jason Arday lifting us up, as Des’ree sings, “love will save the day”.

References and further reading

About the authors

Chantelle Jessica Lewis

Chantelle Jessica Lewis is the Andrew Pitt Junior Research Fellow in Black British Studies at Pembroke College, University of Oxford. She is codirector of Surviving Society Productions, an associate at Leading Routes and Genius Within, and a trustee of the Sociological Review Foundation. 

Read more by Chantelle Jessica Lewis.

Jason Arday

Jason Arday is Professor of Sociology of Education at the University of Cambridge. He is the author of Cool Britannia and Multi-Ethnic Britain (Routledge, 2019) and a trustee of the British Sociological Association. He also sits on the ITV Cultural Advisory Council and is patron of the Adult Literacy Trust.

Read more by Jason Arday.

About the reviewer

Alexa MacDermot

Alexa MacDermot is an autistic PhD student in the School of Social Policy, Social Work and Social Justice at University College Dublin. Her doctoral research focuses on the identities, social relations and employment experiences of autistic and/or ADHD cisgender women, non-binary feminised individuals and transgender women in midlife in Ireland.
