Who Plagiarized More: Jason Arday or Shakespeare from North?

And what is the bigger "cover-up"?

Dennis McCarthy
Jul 24, 2026

As a plagiarism connoisseur, I do have to agree that Professor Jason Arday of Cambridge is one of the more blatant plagiarists in history. Nathan Cofnas, who is race-obsessed and a controverisal figure of the far-right, has indeed confirmed the extent of Arday’s appropriation—while also questioning some of Arday’s more extraordinary biographical claims.1

It seems Cambridge wanted to sweep this under the rug—and “Retraction Watch” has also looked into Arday’s writing and agreed with the plagiarism charge (it’s impossible not to). The article ends with a quote from Martyn Hammersley, a sociologist at Open University:

He told us he believes that Cofnas’s Substack post was just about the worst place for them to emerge. “It’s an unfortunate situation because it could have been dealt with by Cambridge,” he said. “The result of that is that it means that the only people who will take it up are the people on the radical right.”

Regardless, as always, I only look at data, facts, and arguments; and as Cofnas’ identified dozens of stolen passages—and used plagiarism software (Copyleaks) to do it—the story is obviously right up my alley and relevant to the Shakespeare-North discovery.

In the first section, for example, Cofnas provides 36 instances in Arday’s PhD dissertation (2015) in which he plagiarized passages from Paula Zwozdiac-Myers’ dissertation (2009). He also documents other examples in which the professor stole from others for still other papers. Here are Cofnas’ first 2 of the 36 examples.

As the title of the paper suggests, Cofnas also criticized Cambridge for ignoring and downplaying these extensive examples of plagiarism. He describes this, correctly in my view, as a kind of “cover-up.” (I don’t think it is a conspiracy exactly; it is just that people often feel compelled not to admit uncomfortable truths—sometimes, even to themselves.)

My subscribers know exactly where this is heading:

Cofnas is exactly right about the egregiousness of these examples and the shamefulness of Cambridge’s refusal to acknowledge it. But consider this:

  • Imagine that, after Jason Arday plagiarized 36 passages from Zwozdiak-Myers’s dissertation, he went on to publish two more books that likewise plagiarized, to a similarly astonishing extent, from other sections of that same dissertation.

  • Indeed, imagine that virtually every idea, theme, subject, and historical figure discussed across Arday’s first three books comes directly from Zwozdiak-Myers—and that, on the rare occasions he draws from another source, it is simply another work written by Zwozdiak-Myers herself.

  • Now imagine that Cambridge, Oxford, Harvard, Yale, Princeton, and every other major university in the world are presented with this evidence, yet repeatedly insist that such borrowing was entirely normal for his era and genre—that leading intellectuals and celebrated writers routinely plagiarized on this scale as well. Yet this claim is demonstrably false. It is not even remotely true.

  • Imagine further that Arday continues writing books, now borrowing extensively from still more works by Zwozdiak-Myers.

  • Then imagine that, over the course of more than two decades, Arday enjoys a long and prolific career, producing 37 works and eventually being celebrated as the greatest writer in history. Entire university departments are devoted to teaching his writings. Yet in all but one of his works—his worst and least substantial—Arday has plagiarized material from something Zwozdiak-Myers wrote.

  • Indeed, imagine that Arday somehow acquires Zwozdiak-Myers’s private papers and personal journal and borrows extensively from them as well.

  • More astonishing still, imagine that he discovers a book once owned by Zwozdiak-Myers, filled with her handwritten notes in the margins, and then constructs an entire book from those marginal annotations.

  • Finally, imagine that all of these acts of plagiarism have been meticulously documented in peer-reviewed scholarship and covered in major media reports. Imagine that a New York Times bestselling investigative journalist finds the evidence so extraordinary that he spends five years verifying it before publishing an entire book on the subject. And yet, despite all of this, universities and the broader public continue to dismiss the evidence, insisting that everything is perfectly normal.

Well, this certainly would be even more outlandish. Everyone should feel outrage at this discovery—and help try to right this historical injustice and restack this fact. At the very least, they should take time out of their busy days and hit like on the post proving this plagiarism!

Yet as shown below, everything I wrote above is exactly true about Shakespeare’s borrowings from Thomas North. Indeed, no one has borrowed more from a single writer than Shakespeare has from North—likely not one tenth as much. (Indeed, click here to see proof that North’s journal was used in the plays. And click here to see how North wrote the outline to Cymbeline in the margins of a family history book. And the investigative journalist who wrote a book on North and Shakespeare is Michael Blanding: In Shakespeare’ Shadow: A Rogue Scholar’s Quest to Reveal the True Source Behind the World’s Greatest Plays. Finally, for Richard Hanania’s interview of me on Shakespeare, click here.)

As shown in the Asch Conformity experiment, people typically believe their ears, not their eyes. That is, people often accept what others will say—especially those who they deem as trusted sources—even over what they can plainly see with their own eyes. What is shown below—what you can see with your own eyes—are hundreds of passages and lines that Shakespeare clearly took from North. And no, no one else in history—not even the most obsessed and neurotic plagiarists in history—has borrowed half as much.

Just keep scrolling downward, and the extraordinary nature of Shakespeare’s borrowings becomes undeniable. And any effort to downplay them should seem embarrassing.
