Of Plagiarism and Cambridge University

Published by 21percent.org on 22 July 202622 July 2026

Cambridge University’s Professor of the Sociology of Education, Jason Arday, has become the subject of allegations of plagiarism.

Dr Nathan Cofnas has highlighted a number of passages that appear to show close textual similarities between Arday’s work and those of other sociologists in the fields of education and critical race theory, one example of which is reproduced above. Similar concerns about Professor Arday’s work have also been noted previously by other academics (for example, see Prof David Harris here).

Plagiarism is not a victimless crime. It robs original thinkers of recognition, distorts the integrity of scholarship and undermines the trust on which the entire academic community depends. There will be less well-known or junior scholars who missed out on citations, grants or even tenured jobs because they were deprived of the credit rightfully theirs.

However, it is important to focus on institutional processes and standards rather than on any one individual. The central issue is how Cambridge University addresses and manages such concerns.

Worries were first raised in 2023 both with Liverpool John Moores University and Cambridge University regarding Prof Jason Arday’s Ph D thesis and some of his later publications. Two Professors at UK Universities independently wrote to Dr Rhys Morgan, Head of Policy, Integrity and Governance at Cambridge University about possible plagiarism. They both received almost identical replies:

The University of Cambridge takes issues of research integrity very seriously and we will always consider allegations relating to research conducted under its auspices in line with our policies. In this instance the research that your concerns relate to was carried out before Professor Arday joined the University and it would normally be for the institution at which the work was done to consider any research integrity concerns with a particular paper published in their name. As such we do not consider it within our remit to investigate the papers to which you refer. If you do choose to approach the institutions at which this work was done we would be content for you to let them know that you have also written to us and to provide my name as a contact point. [Dr Rhys Morgan]

Whenever an administrator at Cambridge University says, “We take this very seriously,” it’s usually your first clue that they absolutely don’t …

Dr Morgan clings to a flimsy excuse as a cover for not investigating — namely, it’s someone else’s job. This is familiar territory. Dr Morgan has ‘previous’ in the matter of Dr Juan Manuel Gorriz, as reported by For Better Science.

The response is wrong for two reasons.

First, in his application for a Professorship at Cambridge University, Jason Arday will have provided his curriculum vitae and publication list. Therefore, his present prestigious appointment owes much to his earlier work and his PhD thesis. It is inadequate to dismiss the matter as not “within our remit”, as the material was used to acquire his Professorship.

Secondly, past mistakes are of relevance to the future. If a scholar’s research methodology is deficient, then — at minimum — further mentoring, training and advice are needed to ensure that in the future proper standards are upheld. Papers that may have failed to attribute the work of others need either correction with proper attribution or withdrawal. Addressing such issues would also be in the individual’s own professional interest and could reasonably be expected to form part of the University’s oversight and duty of care.

We take no satisfaction in what has happened. Widening participation in higher education remains an important objective. At the same time, maintaining rigorous standards of research integrity and academic culture is absolutely essential.

It is also noted that the 21 Group has received a number of complaints concerning the Policy, Governance and Integrity Section within the Research Strategy Office. This suggests a need for independent external review to assess its effectiveness.

Sadly, Cambridge University’s reaction to anything awkward is to cover it up. This is very dangerous. As Ben Bradlee, executive editor of The Washington Post, famously said “It’s not the crime, it’s the cover up, that gets you.”

Eileen Nugent · 23 July 2026 at 06:45

I think one of the main problems with organisational hiring processes that are not open and transparent to the person being hired is the type of difficulties that such processes can create for a person after a person has been hired to a position in the organisation.

It is possible to have a situation like this: The people doing the hiring are making adjustments to the organisational standards for hiring into a particular position without giving the person being hired any information about the magnitude of any adjustments that are being made to organisational standards with respect to the person being hired into a position.

The same people doing the hiring then expect the exact same standards from that person in a particular position as everyone else in that position in the organisation creating additional pressure on a person – standards gap needs to be closed – in what could already be an extremely high pressure environment.

There is no real thought beyond adjusting the hiring standards, there is no corresponding adjustment of the standards expected of the person in the position. There is no organisational plan to bring the person from one standard – the hiring standard – up to the organisational standard expected nor to adjust the organisational standard expected to match the hiring standard. The person is expected to find a way to close the gap, to meet the same organisational standards as every one else in the same position is expected to meet.

There is then an extra health risk that needs to be acknowledged in this type of situation – it is possible for exceptional mental health pressures to be generated some which needs to be given serious consideration. Wherever there is additional risk in a situation there is potentially additional reward in the situation – a person can become capable of dealing with exceptional mental health pressures & hence become capable of meeting a standard that is well above the expected standard.

If the situation induces a hard fail – falling well below expected standards – it can force a person to make more significant changes, to commit to significant & prolonged efforts at self reform to meet the expected standards, which could mean an even harder fail – falling even further below expected standards – and/or a significant increase in standards the person is able to meet well above the expected standards. This is coupled to additional risk to mental health during this time.

It’s a situation analogous to this one : a person is offered an opportunity to climb Mount Everest by an organisation supporting people to climb Mount Everest where adjustments have been made for the persons climbing skills as compared to the climbing skills of other climbers who have already successfully climbed Mount Everest. The person is then potentially taking on an extra risk in climbing Mount Everest but the person could after climbing Mount Everest have acquired better climbing skills than other climbers who have successfully climbed Mount Everest. It’s an additional physical risk with potential for higher physical reward – physical fitness gains, gains in climbing skills. There are serious trade offs to be done in these situations whilst making gains in physical fitness/climbing skills – what pace do you start at, how often to take breaks, if your health starts to suffer do you push on or turn back, whats happening with the physical environment.

I think people assume if hiring standards are being adjusted that the standards expected of the person in the organisation will also be adjusted but it’s not clear that this is typically the case – it’s not the case in Cambridge. Hiring standards can be adjusted up or down for all sorts of reasons that have very little to do with personal characteristics – favouring internal candidates, increased interest from external candidates – that doesn’t mean the standards expected of the person once in the position are being adjusted. The person is subjected to the same internal and external competition as all the other people in that position in the organisation in Cambridge.

If a person is put under exceptionally high pressure in an organisation – for any reason – and the person subsequently struggles to find ways to cope with that pressure the person could come to rely heavily on others who are more able to cope with the pressures they are under in that organisation. There is then an increased risk of exploitative relationships emerging.

The means it is possible for the emergence of exploitative relationships in academia to have nothing to do with personal characteristics, for these type of relationships can happen between people with the same personal characteristics, for the emergence of these type of relationships to be purely situational. It is not enough to actively resist being exploited by others, it is also necessary to actively resist exploiting others, it is necessary to actively find the right balance in these high pressure situations. The higher the pressure in a situation the more of a challenge it can become to actively find this balance point in a situation.

Equally if a person is put under exceptionally high pressure in an organisation the person could learn individual ways to cope with exceptionally high pressures at which point others could come to rely heavily on that person to cope with the pressures they are under in that organisation. It could even oscillate over time – exploitative relationship in one direction, inversion, exploitative relationship in the other direction. The power imbalance could dynamically shift, it may be necessary to demand equality from others at one point in time and then to give equality to others at another point in time.

It’s a risk-reward situation for both the person and the organisation. It’s a double-edged sword for both individuals & organisations.
