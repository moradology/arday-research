Who's the most pitiful person in the Cambridge University fraud scandal?

It's not who you think.

Andrew Gelman
Jul 28, 2026

Something was bugging me about this story of the sociology professor from Cambridge University who was revealed to have faked his research. (As I wrote earlier, this has been called a plagiarism scandal, but it’s really data fabrication. Plagiarism was just the method he used for the fabrication.)

Here’s my question for you: Who’s the most pitiful, sorry-ass person in this scandal?

It’s not Jason Arday, the now-disgraced professor. He seems to have been a pathological liar, a very disturbed person, the sort of complete fabulist who might be able to succeed in some areas of business, politics, or religion, but ultimately will struggle in the open environment of academia. He flamed out like a firecracker; I wouldn’t call him pitiful.

Yes, Arday’s work is pitiful. Lacking the bold touches of expert fraud publishers such as Brian Wansink, Michael Lacour, and Dan Ariely, it’s just boring. Here’s an example flagged by formerly Cambridge-affiliated academic Nathan Cofnas. The left column comes from Arday (2015), the right from Zwozdiak-Myers (2009):

“The vehicular of mentoring and reflecting practice”? As Kingsley Amis would say, The what of mentoring and reflecting practice? The vehicular of what? As so often is the case, the plagiarism is worse than the original. Arday would’ve produced a better project by just giving the citation and not trying to do this rewriting.

But I don’t think that the production of sorry-ass, pitiful work makes Arday a sorry-ass pitiful person. Unlike Wansink etc., he was unassuming in his scholarly work, but he was flamboyant as a performer and as an inventor of his life story. It’s hard for me to feel angry or sorry at such a person, any more than I would consider Dale Carnegie or some other self-help figure as a sorry creature. Fabrication is their way of being, that’s all.

And the most pitiful figures in the scandal are not the lawyers of Carter-Ruck, who subverted the processes of truth by intimidating the magazine Times Higher Education from running a story on this scandal last year. Yes, these lawyers are evil, but they’re not pitiful: they’re getting paid big bucks for their job. They may be going to hell, but they’re enjoying a comfortable ride on the way there.

Nor is it the editors of Times Higher Education: if you run a magazine in the U.K. that’s not called Private Eye, you sometimes have to spike juicy stories because of their horrible libel laws. It’s just part of the job.

Nor is it the authors who blurbed Arday’s book, which we can only assume is full of lies. I guess maybe they should’ve been suspicious that the book had a ghostwriter—a sociology professor who does qualitative interviewing should be able to write his own story, no?—but giving these blurbs is just a temporary embarrassment. Everybody makes mistakes, and more famous people have blurbed worse books than that.

The most pitiful, sorry-ass person in this scandal is not Dave Harris, the person who first seems to have noticed the research fraud, nor is it Anjum Memum, the source of the words that Arday misattributed, or Martyn Hammersley, the sociologist who followed up on this with Cambridge University, or the aforementioned Nathan Cofnas, who looked into Arday’s fabrications in a variety of settings. All these people had frustrating experiences in one way or another, but they’re not suffering any personal or professional consequences from their efforts in exposing this scandal, and they have the satisfaction of now seeing it all come out.

Hilary Cremin and Rhys Morgan, the Cambridge administrators who received and then dismissed the original reports of research misconduct, acted improperly, and they should be subject to embarrassment. But they’re not going to get fired. At worst, they’ll lose their titles and some administrative roles--maybe Cremin will step down as the Head of the Faculty of Education at the university--but they’ll still be living comfortably. So they’re not the sorry-ass, pitiful people in the story either. Cremin also called Arday a “world-class scholar,” which seems like an erroneous assessment, but, again, she won’t lose her job over this or even take a pay cut.

Similarly, Pro-Vice-Chancellor for Education Bhaskar Vira is not the most pitiful person in this story: yes, Vira described Arday as “an exceptional scholar”--an inaccurate description, unless “exceptional” includes fraud as part of its ambit--but, again, I suspect that this well-paid administrator face no consequences for this dereliction of duty. To be fair, this might not be Vira’s fault at all, as this assessment could well come from a summary of some committee report.

Whoever at Cambridge University made the decision to hire the notorious Carter-Ruck law firm . . . that’s a dastardly deed, but I guess that person’s job is to hire tough lawyers. And, again, the people at Cambridge who get to decide whether to hire a law firm are probably well-paid university executive themselves, insulated from any consequences of their bad decisions.

But we’re getting closer.

From the Retraction Watch report:

A spokesperson for Cambridge told Retraction Watch that Arday was a “victim of a vile campaign to undermine his credibility” and that he had been cleared of “allegations of plagiarism regarding his thesis and journal publications.”

Bing bing bing! This is my nomination for the most pitiful, sorry-ass person in the entire story: whoever is the “spokesperson for Cambridge” who gave them that statement.

Think about it:

  1. To say such a thing requires a complete lack of self-respect, a willingness to tell an immediately refutable lie in public. This spokesperson is in the position of Winston Smith saying 2 + 2 = 5, or a press officer having to make sense of whatever stupid lie a politician said that day.

  2. But, unlike Winston Smith, the spokesperson is not under physical threat, and, unlike the press officer, the spokesperson can’t pretend to justify the lying as serving some larger political purpose. Loyalty to Cambridge University is one thing, but loyalty to some colorful asshole (sorry, I forgot my English; that should be “colourful arsehole”) who fabricated his data? That’s no principle at all.

  3. And, unlike the various lawyers and administrators mentioned above, this spokesperson is presumably not well-paid.

So, here’s a person who has to lie in public, with no good excuse for doing so, and doesn’t even get compensated for the moral sacrifice. That’s a truly pitiful person.

That said, it’s not too late for this spokesperson to quit and write a tell-all memoir about life in the public relations department at Cambridge University. If that happens, I’ll be first on line to buy the book. And I’ll take back what I said about the person being pitiful.

Just to be clear, the spokesperson is not the person here who angers me the most. I’m most angered by the lawyers at Carter-Ruck and the smug university administrators who keep passing the buck. I don’t like the spokesperson either--there’s no excuse for blatantly lying like that, nor for attacking (as “vile”) the people who pointed out the research fraud--but we have to be madder at the higher-ups here, however much we despise the lesser-paid liars who do their bidding.

P.S. You might ask, why write two posts on this episode?

Yes, Cambridge is a famous university, but it’s not famous for its sociology department, and this particular professor is pretty obscure. Yes, he’d been in the news occasionally, but I’d never heard of him before, and the blurbers of his book were not particularly famous, which indicates to me that he’s not so well connected.

The answer to this question is as follows. First, it’s kind of a crazy story, this guy who fabricated not just his research but also much of his life story. I once knew a pathological liar, and it’s a stunning thing to see. So that’s why I wrote the first post. As for the post you’re reading right now, I care about moral questions. The question, “Who’s the most sorry-ass, pitiful person in this story” is particularly interesting in how it combines moral and social issues. I’m not asking who’s the most immoral, but who’s the one who elicits the least respect. A mentally disturbed person such as Arday or a sociopath such as whoever works at Carter-Ruck, these sorts of people can cause lots of damage, but low-level employees who degrade themselves like this, that makes me sad in a different way.
