‘Black Excellence’ as a Fragile Construct: Jason Arday, Racialised Scrutiny, and the Unequal Politics of Mistakes

July 25, 2026Critical Race Theory, diversity, Employment, Gender, Leadership, Narratives, Privilege, Race, Racial literacy, Racism, Research, Unconscious bias, Whiteness, workplaceAnti-racism, Black men, Racism, UniversitiesPenny Rabiger

I want to write about Jason Arday’s recent plagiarism controversy, not least because it is noticeably stark how the fascist right will be using this as proof that he was a ‘diversity hire’ and the liberal anti-racists will be claiming it is just an attack because he is Black. And of course, equally noticeable will be all those that rushed to have their photo taken with him or to claim him as an example of ‘Black excellence’ who are now nowhere to be seen.

What I want to highlight most here is that Jason Arday’s recent plagiarism controversy is more than an academic integrity story. It exposes how Black men in elite institutions are often turned into symbols of possibility and then, at the first serious stumble, recast as symbols of failure. The issue is not even whether academic standards should matter, but whether those standards are applied consistently, proportionately, and without racial double standards. And more importantly, whether we have the racial literacy to accurately see, name and resist the racial regimes which underpin all of it.

A story bigger than one allegation
The public framing of Arday long before this allegation has been striking because it has not been limited to questions of citation and authorship. He has also been represented through the language of exceptionalism: youngest, first, breakthrough, diversity figure, even ‘autistic savant’. That kind of visibility can be a form of achievement, but it can also make Black academics more exposed when controversy arrives. The same symbolic weight that elevates a person can also make collapse feel more dramatic, more moral, and more total.

Black men in academia are never encountered as neutral individuals. They are often read through race before they are read through scholarship, and through representation before they are read through complexity. When something goes wrong, the response can move very quickly from a problem to be investigated to proof of fraud, with far less room for context, due process, or proportionality.

What is publicly reported
One way to discuss Arday is to stay close to what has been publicly reported: that he has been accused of plagiarism and that the matter has become the subject of intense commentary. It should be pointed out that this is not the same thing as a final adjudication of wrongdoing. And in a way, whether he made a mistake or not is by the by for my analysis. What should be noted is the speed and tone of the reaction, as they are themselves revealing, because they show how quickly a Black academic can become a lightning rod for wider anxieties about merit, race, and institutional legitimacy.

The press coverage has also done something we should all recognise by racialising the case while claiming to simply describe it. Headlines that stress his position as the “youngest Black professor” or “diversity poster boy” do more than identify him. They invite the reader to interpret his alleged mistake not only as an individual breach, but as a test of Black legitimacy in the academy. That is a familiar pattern, and it is part of what makes the case culturally significant.

Intersectionality, race and visibility
Research on UK higher education repeatedly shows that racism in universities is not simply a matter of overt exclusion. It also appears through surveillance, exclusion from informal networks, tokenism, being over-scrutinised, and being held to hyper-demanding standards of proof and performance. Black academics describe the pressure to outperform non-Black colleagues, while also being treated as if their achievements are more fragile, less secure, and more easily discredited. In that context, a single controversy can become a career-defining event in ways that are disproportionate to the pattern we see around white men in comparable positions.

This is where race and gender intersect. Black men in elite spaces are often expected to embody a very narrow form of respectability. They are permitted to be inspiring, exceptional, and useful to institutional branding, but not always to be fallible. When they make mistakes, those mistakes may be interpreted not as human error but as evidence that they never deserved their status in the first place.

Arday’s case is further complicated by the intersection of autism, Blackness, and a working-class background. Public narratives about him have often leaned on an overcoming-adversity script. He was the child who did not speak until eleven, did not read or write until eighteen, and then rose to the heights of academia. While this may be intended as inspirational, it can also tip into an ableist trope in which extraordinary achievement is framed as surprising precisely because the subject is imagined as otherwise deficient. That framing is especially loaded for a Black autistic man from a working-class background, because it can collapse disability, race, and class into a single story of astonishment or exception rather than a fuller account of structural support, uneven access, and neurodiverse ways of knowing.

That is why the response to Arday should be understood through a critical lens. The issue is not only plagiarism; it is the social life of plagiarism when attached to a Black man whose success has already been made public as an institutional showcase.

Class and legitimacy
Class sharpens the picture further. Elite institutions often reward ease with dominant cultural codes such as accent, style, manner, confidence, inherited familiarity, with what counts as proper scholarship, and fluency in unwritten rules. Black men who enter those spaces may be expected to prove legitimacy more aggressively than white peers (while never, ever being actually aggressive, which is strictly forbidden for Black men), and they may also be punished more harshly when they appear to violate norms. The result is that their place in the institution can feel conditional in a way that white male privilege often is not.

Research on Black men in academia and postgraduate study shows that they frequently experience racialised isolation, pressure to represent, and a constant need to negotiate hostile or suspicious environments. They may be told they are exceptional when useful, but treated as suspect when vulnerable. This is not just a matter of personal feeling. It shapes careers, promotions, and the public interpretation of professional controversy and is widely researched, documented and written about. A classed idea of who belongs in elite knowledge spaces often sits just beneath the surface of these judgements.

Unequal scandal
We only have to contrast Arday’s treatment with a handful of high profile powerful white men and how they are treated in much more extreme and public cases, to understand how this imbalance works. Tony Blair remains an example of how elite status can survive moral controversy, even where public outrage is intense and long-lasting. He is still Sir Tony Blair, and whatever one thinks of his legacy, his honours and continued stature show that scandal and even being a war criminal, does not always strip white elite men of legitimacy. The same is true, in a different register, of Donald Trump, who has faced civil fraud findings and criminal conviction, yet remains the current president of the USA. He has also long been associated with openly degrading and misogynistic language ( a self-professed ‘Pussy Grabber’), yet that has not produced the kind of durable exclusion that many Black professionals face after far smaller missteps.

The point here is not that Blair and Trump are morally identical, or that their cases can be mapped neatly onto Arday’s. The point is that power mediates consequences. Powerful white men often retain access, status, and platforms even after grave misconduct, while Black men can be pushed toward reputational ruin through allegation, insinuation, and symbolic association. That asymmetry should trouble anyone interested in racial justice or simply in fairness. We should all be looking around our own organisations and noting the ways that some people with the right level of racialised, classed and gendered protections seem to be coated in Teflon, able to keep rising despite being obviously underqualified, inexperienced and more worryingly, inept, bullies, sexual predators and worse.

There are of course also plenty of examples of white male academics who have been accused of plagiarism or academic misconduct, and who continued on regardless. For example, prominent professor of immunology, Quentin Sattentau was accused of severe “author misconduct” and plagiarism by a senior scientist in his laboratory, Dr. Amin Moghaddam. The accusation, which eventually went to a public employment tribunal, alleged a calculated approach by the professor to steal research ideas and credit while omitting the originating scientist from publication authorship. The internal complaints did not result in disciplinary termination for the senior academic. While the subordinate scientist’s employment eventually ended after funding expired, Professor Sattentau retained his high-profile academic chair and research laboratory at Oxford’s Department of Pathology. At no point did anyone question his intelligence, his legitimacy, whether he had reached his position on merit, or had over-egged his capacity to do his job.

The place of academic integrity
Those comfortable with narratives that suggest that Arday somehow made his way to the top without full legitimacy, should also turn to all those who advised him along the way because he fulfilled a criteria of inclusion, rather than because they had his best interests at heart. Based on what we know about white leadership in white institutions, this is often done in race evasive and ignorant ways, by colleagues who want to demonstrate they are allies but don’t have the racial literacy to truly understand how they could protect Arday well – including from themselves and the white supremacy culture of the institution. If you truly want to protect a Black man on the rise, and ensure that every t is crossed and every i dotted in the most collegial and racially literate way possible, knowing about how easily racialised over-scrutiny can kill even the most dedicated and hard-working is a must. How do you caution and support, without also sounding like a cynic or that you are trying to hold an ambitious young man back?

Obviously, critiquing racial double standards is not the same as excusing plagiarism. Academic integrity matters. Universities need robust standards, and claims about authorship or citation deserve proper examination. But consistency matters. As does how ‘standards’ are written with whiteness as the benchmark, and how they are used as a tool to maintain the status quo of whiteness as the norm in institutions. If institutions demand accountability, they should also apply context, due process, and proportionality without racialised overreaction. This will never happen in a university system which is race evasive, driven by white supremacy culture and where liberal anti-racism is more of a hindrance than a help.

That distinction is crucial because anti-racist criticism is often misread as special pleading. It is not. The argument is not that Black men should be exempt from scrutiny. It is that scrutiny is not distributed evenly, and that the public punishment of Black men often carries symbolic weight beyond the facts of the case. Once a Black man in an elite institution is cast as a failure, the story can become less about the alleged misconduct and more about the supposed impossibility of his belonging.

And if we are to speak of academic integrity, it must be noted who is behind some of the accusations of Arday’s work. Dr. Nathan Cofnas is an explicit defender of eugenics, and has published work specifically aimed at rehabilitating the concept. Cofnas advocates for the idea that there are biological and genetic differences in IQ and traits between different ethnic groups. His public writing (including arguments that institutions like Harvard would have almost no Black professors under a purely merit-based system) provoked widespread protests, petitions, and demands for his firing from the Cambridge University and College Union (UCU). While the university cleared him, Emmanuel College terminated his research fellowship. Cofnas sued the college for discrimination based on philosophical belief, but a court ultimately ruled in favour of Emmanuel College’s right to sever ties.

Who is allowed complexity and who becomes essentialised?
The deeper issue is not one academic controversy. It is how institutions, media, and publics decide who is allowed complexity. White men with power are often permitted mess, contradiction, and redemption, while Black men are more often flattened into moral narratives of rise and fall. When they succeed, they are made to stand for progress; when they stumble, they are made to stand for hypocrisy. That is a racialised burden, and it is intensified by classed assumptions about who is entitled to authority in the first place, and further complicated by the “Autistic savant” narratives surrounding Arday’s biography.

A genuinely anti-racist approach would insist on three things at once: the seriousness of ‘standards’ and the seriousness of unequal treatment, and also the reality of the system of academia which, as a good friend and long-time academic said to me, “makes this kind of behaviour almost compulsory”. It would reject both impunity and racialised spectacle. And it would ask why some people can survive scandal with honours intact, while others are made to carry the full weight of institutional shame.

That is the real significance of the Arday case. It is not only about what one scholar is alleged to have done. It is about how quickly Black men can be turned from symbols of achievement into symbols of collapse, and how much that says about race, class, gender, and disability in contemporary public life.
