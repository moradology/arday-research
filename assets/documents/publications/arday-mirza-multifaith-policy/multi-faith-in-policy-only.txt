Introduction
In 2010, ‘religion or belief ’ became one of nine characteristics protected under the Equality
Act (2010). This Act consolidated previous legislation related to race, sex and disability,
and expanded it to age, sexual orientation, marriage and civil partnership, religion or belief,
gender reassignment and pregnancy and maternity. Subsequently, UK universities have
been expected to ensure equality of opportunity, promote good relations and eliminate
harassment for those sharing these protected characteristics. Yet the attention universities
give to religion or belief equality, forging good relations across religious differences, and
eliminating religion-related harassment varies. For example, some universities have exten-
sive resources for religious students, encompassing religious diets, holidays, prayer space,
chaplaincy and religious student societies. At others, provision is patchy. This article draws
on survey data collected in 2022 from 4618 students at 133 UK universities. We examine

Equality Act’s three foci (eliminating harassment; equal opportunities; and good relations),
comparing differences between religious groups and different types of university. We will
discuss these findings through the lens of equality/inequality, arguing that despite the posi-

   The growth of religious diversity in the UK, the new statistical evidence of the size of
religious communities, and the Employment Equality (Religion or Belief) Regulations 2003
informed the context for introducing the Equality Act (2010) (Aspinall 2000). The Act
accorded new protection to ‘religion or belief ’ groups, although the reaction to the legisla-
tion by religious groups was, as Vickers (2011b) predicted, not universally positive (Purdam
et al. 2017).
   Equality statistics have been collected in higher education in England since at least 2010
(OfS 2022, 15–16) and are included in Advance HE’s annual reports. Evidence from Weller,
Hooley, and Moore (2011) helped persuade HESA in 2012–2013 to request universities to
collect data on religion and belief (Weller and Hooley 2017); data collection became man-
datory from 2017 to 2018 (OfS 2022, 15, 22), with collection rates rising. The Office for
Students’ data from 2020 to 2021 on student characteristics show that 46.2% of undergrad-
uate entrants said they had no religion, 32.1% said they were Christian and 13.0% identified
as Muslim; postgraduates were slightly more likely to report no religion (47.1%) than under-
graduates (OfS 2022, 9).


Literature review
There has been a notable neglect of religion in UK higher education research (Stevenson
2014), but this is changing. This article contributes to this growing body of research.
Research reviewed below relates to the three foci of the Equality Act, indicating how far
universities are eliminating discrimination and harassment, advancing equality of oppor-
tunity and fostering good relations. The research reported below focuses on religion and
belief among students; the topic of religion and belief amongst staff requires separate
investigation.
   Several caveats are needed. While statistics on discrimination and harassment give cause
for concern, most survey data featured below is gathered from volunteer samples. This
sampling strategy does not account for the ways experience of harassment may influence
one’s response rate, making generalisation challenging. Second, what students consider to
be harassment may not be the same as what university or legal investigations might establish
has taken place. Third, marginalisation can coexist with progress towards inclusion. As
Hopkins (2011) observed through analysis of interviews with 29 Muslim students at one
university, students emphasise both the liberal and tolerant nature of the university and the
discrimination or exclusion they face.


Discrimination and harassment research
A survey undertaken by Weller, Hooley, and Moore (2011) for the Equality Challenge
Unit (ECU, now incorporated into Advance HE) found 6.1% of 3935 students reporting
                                               British Journal of Sociology of Education      3


discrimination or harassment on the grounds of religion or belief. This figure was higher
for Jewish (27%), Sikh (17%) and Muslim students (14%). However, less than 1.7% of
students had made a formal complaint about this. A survey of 925 Jewish students con-
ducted around the same time found that a fifth said they had been subjected to antisem-
itism that academic year, and a further third had witnessed an antisemitic incident on
campus (Graham and Boyd 2011). Jewish, Muslim and Sikh students were most likely to
have experienced such hate incidents. Aune et al.’s (2020) survey of students at one UK
university found that 6.5% of 612 students surveyed said that they had, since coming to
university, personally experienced a religion-related hate incident/crime. A contempo-
raneous nationwide study found a ‘climate of prejudice’ against Muslims across UK uni-
versities, fuelled by entrenched stereotypes, often perpetuated by mass media, and
government counter-terrorism measures which reinforced suspicion among students and
staff (Scott-Baumann et al. 2020, 131). The experience of Muslim students in further and
higher education was investigated by the NUS (2018), who found a third (of 578) had
experienced abuse or crime at their institution, with one in five subjected to verbal abuse.
Echoing Hopkins’ (2011) point, Saeed’s interviews with 40 Muslim students reveal that
positive work to counter harassment coexists with Islamophobia in UK universities (Saeed
2018). Before the events of 7 October 2023, the Community Security Trust reported a
22% increase in on-campus antisemitic incidents compared to two years previously; most
were online and spiked when the Israel-Palestine conflict escalated (Harris and
Goldberg 2023).


Advancing equality of opportunity research
Weller, Hooley, and Moore studied university data collection on religion. Over 80% of
students said they would disclose the information. Buddhists and Pagans were less com-
fortable disclosing, possibly due to concerns about state surveillance (Weller and Hooley
2017, 42–43). Around 10% of students disagreed or strongly disagreed that the course
teaching or content ‘is sensitive to my religion or belief ’ (Weller and Hooley 2017, 48).
    Religious calendars can conflict with the calendars of UK universities. Whether univer-
sities make allowances to enable students to celebrate religious festivals when they conflict
with teaching or exams varies (Weller, Hooley, and Moore 2011). Non-Christian students
may feel excluded by Christian elements in university ceremonies, for example, prayer at
graduation. Religious students may feel likewise marginalised when universities exclusively
champion secular or secularist values (Weller and Hooley 2017).
    Religious barriers to financing higher education are highlighted by Avdukic et al. (2023),
who discuss Somali students influenced by interpretations of some Shari’ah scholars that
interest-bearing loans constitute usury (ribā). If Somali students see their religion as pro-
hibiting taking out interest-bearing loans—and they find some do—this indicates that the
government has failed to create a student finance system equally accessible to all groups.1
    Miles and Benn (2016) discuss the extent to which one university’s sports activities are
equally accessible to Muslim women. The mixed-sex organisation of many and the domi-
nance of male coaches present barriers due to Islamic expectations around modesty.
    Research addressing whether students of particular religions are less likely to reach their
academic potential finds a significant Muslim awarding gap; Muslims are the religious
group least likely to graduate with a first-class or upper second-class degree (Codiroli

Mcmaster 2020). Some explain this in terms of Islamophobia or conflate Islamophobia with
racism, but Gholami (2021) points to the intersection of religion and race.
   Chaplaincy is an important religious resource in universities. Research undertaken by
Aune et al. (2019) found that there are around 1000 chaplains in UK universities (2019, 11);
the average per institution is over 10, but almost two-thirds are volunteers. The proportion
of chaplains from minority faiths increased in the period 2007 to 2017, from 30% to 37%,
with numbers of Muslim (9%) and Jewish chaplains (8%) largest. A minority of universities
have a Sikh, Hindu, Pagan or Humanist chaplain. The majority of chaplains (63%) are
Christian, down from 70% in 2006–2007, but the picture is diversifying.
   Chaplaincies are also increasingly called ‘multi-faith’– a rise from 1 in 10 in 2006–2007
to 1 in 4 universities using this title in 2016–2017 (Aune et al. 2019, 31). In terms of faith
spaces, the average university has 4.9 prayer spaces, and just over two-thirds have at least
one permanent Muslim prayer space, a negligible rise since 2007. 59% have at least one
chapel. (Aune et al. 2019, 31–35). Hopkins’s (2011) study of Muslim students notes that the
discrimination they face is often spatial, with the university mosque located on the periphery
of campus.


Fostering good relations research
Social relations between religious and non-religious students can be hampered by the
­presence of alcohol in social life. Weller, Hooley and Moore’s 2011 research found ‘groups
 of students whose attitudes towards alcohol consumption are defined by their religion or
 belief ’ (Weller, Hooley, and Moore, 2011, 49) more likely to feel unable to participate in
 social activities.
    Stevenson’s (2014, 56) interviews with 15 religious students in a post-1992 university
 found that students experienced being ‘othered’ because of their religious differences, with
 Christian students ridiculed for not having sex and Muslims for presumed association with
 terrorism or women’s oppression. A Jewish student was not given time off to celebrate Jewish
 festivals. Muslims or Sikhs who wore symbols of their faith felt particularly isolated. This
 isolation contrasted with the university’s public multicultural and internationalisation ethos
 and led them to socialise outside the university. Stevenson argues that universities should
 do more to create cross-cultural and interreligious dialogue; having an internationalisation
 strategy is not enough to ensure that students relate well on the ground.
    Aune, Perfect and Ryan’s qualitative study of religious student societies found they offer
 vital support and friendship among religious minorities (Aune et al. 2025). They analyse
 this in terms of bonding and bridging social capital: students bond with likeminded others
 as they find support for their religion in a sometimes-hostile environment, but they do
 much less ‘bridging’ to others, for example, through interfaith activities. Sheldon’s (2016)
 research on the Israel-Palestine conflict at UK universities highlighted how combative
 debates can cause relations between groups to be damaged and contribute to the reinforce-
 ment of stereotypes.
    The literature above shows that inequalities remain in the three areas of the Equality
 Act. Yet almost all the research was conducted before 2020, and except for one project, none
 considered these areas in relation to the Equality Act, or in relation to each other. The one
 project that did so, led by Dinham on religious literacy leadership in higher education,
 interviewed Vice Chancellors, university managers, staff and students about four policy
                                                  British Journal of Sociology of Education          5


arenas: equalities and diversity; widening participation and social mobility; student expe-
rience; and fostering good campus relations. Senior managers had paid religion new atten-
tion, seeing it as part of ‘an “equality and diversity” brief ’ (Dinham and Jones 2012, 192).
They saw becoming more attentive to students’ faith as integral to their increased focus on
the student experience. Some as preferable to focusing on preventing extremism saw fos-
tering good relations, but interviewees regretted that there was sometimes a reluctance by
student societies to work with chaplains. Dinham and Jones’ findings cover some of the
same ground as this article, but ours focuses on student experiences, uses national survey
data, and looks in greater detail at the three aspects of the Equality Act.


Religion and belief inequalities, the Equality Act (2010) and policy
implementation in higher education
Within the Equality Act, the Public Sector Equality Duty (Section 149) came into force in
2011 (Equality Act 2010). The vast majority of universities, like police or government depart-
ments, are public authorities so are required, ‘to have due regard to the need to achieve the
objectives set out under s149 of the Equality Act 2010’ to:

a. Eliminate discrimination, harassment, victimisation and any other conduct that is
   ­prohibited by or under the Equality Act 2010;
b. Advance equality of opportunity between persons who share a relevant protected
    ­characteristic and persons who do not share it;
c. Foster good relations between persons who share a relevant protected characteristic and
     persons who do not share it.

On (b), the duty explains that:
   Having due regard to the need to advance equality of opportunity … involves having due
   regard, in particular, to the need to –

   (c) encourage persons who share a relevant protected characteristic to participate in public
   life or in any other activity in which participation by such persons is disproportionately low.
   (Vickers 2011a, 43–44)
   As Vickers (2011a, 54) points out, this emphasises ‘the idea of equality as inclusion and
participation’ and moves beyond mere equality of opportunity. The term ‘due regard’ does
not commit public authorities to a particular outcome, so arguably lends itself to a ‘tick box’
approach, but ‘to the need’ strengthens the obligations on public authorities (Vickers 2011b,
156). The Equality Act 2010 (Specific Duties) Regulations 2011 require public authorities
to publish ‘equality objectives, at least every four years’ and ‘information to demonstrate
their compliance with the public sector equality duty’.
   The ECU’s (2012, 24) briefing for colleges and HEIs highlights that: ‘Religion or belief
should therefore be taken to mean the full diversity of religious and belief affiliations within
the UK, including non-religious and philosophical beliefs such as atheism, agnosticism and
humanism’.
   As we present data below on students’ experiences of religion or belief-related equality
in universities, two things should be considered. First, the Equality Act coexists alongside
other legislation universities must abide by, such as The Prevent Duty within the
6     K. AUNE ET AL.


Counter-Terrorism and Security Act 2015 and the Higher Education (Freedom of Speech)
Act 2023, and these have different emphases, sometimes pulling universities in different
directions. Second, although the Equality Act is ostensibly positive because it promotes
action to address discrimination and marginalisation of students, law and policy can have
unintended consequences: as Scott-Baumann et al. (2020) show, the counter-terrorism
Prevent Duty has had a negative impact on Muslims, many of whom have felt unfairly
targeted, monitored and stigmatised as a ‘suspect community’.
    Policy implementation in universities, as anywhere, is complex. As Elgström (2000)
shows in the case of gender equality being ‘mainstreamed’ in development and foreign aid
policies in the EU, new norms must ‘fight their way into institutional thinking’, inhibited
by their different interpretation among different actors. ‘Norm diffusion’ can occur, with
widespread internalisation, but there is also ‘norm resistance’. Elgström highlights the role
of ‘norm entrepreneurs’ who advocate for new norms, involving subsequent negotiations
with co-actors. Norm adoption may be ‘formal’ (even if only lip service is paid) or ‘real’,
internalized and adopted throughout the organisation (Elgström 2000, 458–59).
    Ahmed’s work sheds further light on why EDI officers, the group most likely to be tasked
with Equality Act implementation, may have been ineffective in furthering religion and
belief equality, arguing that the focus given to producing race equality documentation
conceals the university’s problems of racism. Ahmed (2007) considers the Race Relations
Amendment Act (2000), which made the promotion of race equality a duty for public bodies
as it is today within the Equality Act. With public bodies required to have a race equality
policy and action plan, Ahmed (2007, 591) examines how policies ‘get translated into action’
in universities. Despite the recommendation to produce the race equality document via
working groups, most higher education institutions tasked someone, often a newly appointed
race equality or HR officer, to write it. It circulates, posted on a website or given to new
staff, but ‘the dissemination of the document does not necessarily do anything’ (594).
    Instead, Ahmed argues that these documents act as a kind of performance: ‘They are
ways in which universities perform an image of themselves, and they are also ways in which
universities perform in the sense of “doing well’’’ (2007, 594). The existence of race equality
documents becomes the evidence that educational institutions are achieving race equality,
when the racism that necessitated them has not gone away. In this way, the documents, and
the diversity languages within them, can actually ‘conceal[s] racism’ (606). Indeed, the
EHRC’s (2019) inquiry into racial harassment in publicly funded universities reveals that
it is still common. Ahmed concludes: ‘the idea that the document “does race” means that
people can think that race has been “done”’ (599). Equality documents should be read
critically to look for the gap between rhetoric and practice, and in this way, the gap between
what they say and what they do can be revealed. We return to these arguments in the
Discussion.


Methods
This article considers student survey data (n = 4618) from The Interfaith Diversity
Experiences and Attitudes Longitudinal Survey (IDEALS UK), collected in Autumn 2022.
After ethical approval was obtained from the University [name and number to be added],
the survey was conducted by the youth research agency YouthSight, which applied a quota
sampling method to an existing panel of young adults based across the UK. The IDEALS

women studying STEM subjects are over-represented (see Table 1). Generalized ranking

percentages found in the UK HE population. This was done using gender, ethnicity, age,

   Population estimates and 95% confidence intervals were generated using the survey
package in the software R (Lumley et al. 2024). Unweighted analysis is provided in the
(Supplementary Material Tables S1–S6). Each variable was analysed by sub-groups relating
to religion and university type, and all comparisons are significant (p < 0.05), unless other-
wise stated. The specific sub-groups were:

• Religion: respondents’ religious identity reported according to HESA (2024) categories:
  No religion, Buddhist, Christian, Hindu, Jewish, Muslim, Sikh, Other. ‘Other’ includes
  agnostic students, plus a range of other perspectives, including Jainism, Shintoism, and
  Spiritualism. At times, small sample sizes mean Buddhist, Hindu, Jewish, Muslim and
  Sikh are combined into a category ‘Minority Religion’.
• University Type: we adopt an institutional typology from Guest et al. (2013) related to
  the founding period, institutional ethos, student demographics and geographical loca-
  tion. This presents five types of university: traditional elite, red brick, 1960s campus,
  new or ‘post-1992’ and Cathedrals Group (CG). Guest et al. (2013) and Aune et al. (2023)
  find differences by type in how universities approach religion, with traditional elite and
  Cathedrals Group giving Christianity, and sometimes religion in general, a privileged
  structural position (e.g., graduation services in religious buildings, better chaplaincy
  provision). This research also revealed similarities in religious identification and engage-
  ment among red brick, 1960s and new universities, so in this article, we use a threefold
  typology: traditional elite, secular (red brick, 1960s campus, and new universities com-
  bined) and the Cathedrals Group.


Findings
Harassment
Our findings suggest that the majority (77.1%) of students have never been mistreated
on campus because of their worldview or religion. Almost nine-in-ten non-religious
students report never experiencing this type of harassment and mistreatment. In con-
trast, religious students report higher levels of mistreatment, with only 62.2% of
Christians and 68.0% of those from minority religions reporting never experiencing
mistreatment (Table 3).
   Of the 22.9% of students who have experienced some form of mistreatment, the majority
have experienced this rarely (9.3%) or occasionally (7.6%). This pattern holds across dif-
ferent religious and non-religious groups, although 10.9% of Christians and 7.0% of those
from minority religions report experiencing mistreatment on campus frequently or all of
the time.
   Our evidence suggests that these experiences of mistreatment are more prevalent at

   Unlike previous studies (e.g. Weller, Hooley, and Moore 2011), we do not find a large
difference between Christian students compared to other religions—in fact, contrary to
studies that find minority religious students experience more harassment, we find a
slightly higher proportion of Christians reporting mistreatment and receiving insensitive
comments about their religion, however the latter finding was statistically non-significant.
Because some minority religious subgroups, including Jewish students, were too small
to reach definitive conclusions, our findings should not be used to argue that all minority
religious groups experience less harassment than Christians. Indeed, surveys, which
include higher numbers of Jewish students report that they are subject to high rates of
harassment (Graham and Boyd 2011; Weller, Hooley, and Moore 2011). There is also the
possibility that, as the proportion of students who are Christian is declining and as an
experience of minority status is more common, these students feel more marginalised.
Moreover, while non-religious students report being on the receiving end of negative
comments less than religious ones, that over half of them have experienced insensitive
comments about their worldview demonstrates that non-religious worldviews also attract
negativity from others, including from staff (see Bowman 2017 on harassment of
non-religious students in the US).


Equal opportunities
Turning to students’ experiences of equal opportunities, we first looked at the inequalities
in students reporting that their university was a safe place for them to express their worl-
dview (see Table 5). Our evidence presents a largely positive picture, with very few students
(6.8%) disagreeing, and almost three quarters (74.2%) agreeing. There were relatively small
differences between different religious groups and between different university types.
    It is notable that a substantial minority (19.1%) report ‘Neither Agree nor Disagree’. The
meaning of this response is difficult to interpret, but it could indicate that some students
felt that their university was sometimes a safe place and sometimes not; that some places
felt safe and others did not; or that they did not openly express their worldview at university.
Given this, caution should be applied so as not to over-interpret the low number of students
reporting explicit disagreement with the statement.
    Similar findings arise from the question of whether faculty and staff accommodate stu-
dents’ needs around celebrating holidays or other observances connected to their worldview
(Table 6). For example, only 6.4% of students felt that their needs were not accommodated
in this way. However, students of minority religions (9.9%) were more likely to disagree
with this statement than Christians (5.9%) or those of no religion (5.8%). This is unsur-
prising given Weller, Hooley and Moore’s (2011) finding that as most universities organise
their calendars in accordance with the western Christian one, it is difficult for some students
to celebrate their own sacred events. The differences between students from different uni-
versity types were relatively small.

Good relations
The final component of inequalities is good relations. We begin by considering how often stu-
dents felt silenced from sharing their experiences with prejudice and discrimination at university
(Table 7). While around six-in-ten students have never experienced this silencing and two-in-ten
have experienced this rarely, 21.0% of students experience silencing on an occasional or more
frequent basis. Religious students are particularly likely to report this experience − 50.7% of
Christians and 46.6% of students from minority religions - compared with 31.1% of those with
no religion. These findings provide statistical evidence that bolsters the silencing described in
qualitative studies by Stevenson (2014) and Scott-Baumann et al. (2020). Similarly, when we
consider those experiencing silencing frequently or all of the time, this applied to 13.1% of
Christians, 8.6% of those from minority religions, and 3.9% of those with no religion.
   In terms of university type, the pattern of silencing echoes the findings related to harass-
ment. Silencing appeared to be more commonplace at traditional elite institutions, with
one-in-two students experiencing this, compared with around 38.4% and 26.9% of students
from secular and Cathedrals Group universities, respectively. Those reporting silencing
frequently or all the time made up 12.0% at traditional elite universities, compared with
7.0% at secular and 5.8% at Cathedrals Group institutions.
   The second aspects of good relations concerns whether students have experienced a
‘provocative encounter’, i.e. one in which students critically examine their own worldviews
in relation to other worldviews (Mayhew et al. 2024). Previous research suggests provocative
encounters are positively associated with the generation of more positive attitudes towards
those of other religions (Mayhew et al. 2024; Peacock et al. 2023). In our data, there is a
largely positive picture. While provocative encounters are not generally experienced fre-
quently, the majority of students, 79.0%, have experienced a provocative encounter at some
point at university (see Table 8). Of these, 58.4% experienced this in the classroom, 76.7%
outside of class.
   Our findings suggest that Christian students (85.6%) are the most likely to experience
provocative encounters, compared with their peers from minority religions (76.3%) or with
no religion (75.6%). These Christian students were particularly likely to experience this
within the classroom (67.7%) compared with their peers from minority religions (51.5%)
and those with no religion (54.7%). Those at traditional elite universities were the most
likely to experience these provocative encounters (89.8%), compared with secular (77.3%)
and Cathedrals Group (74.7%) universities.

Discussion
Our findings that nearly two-thirds of students have experienced an insensitive comment
about their worldview and more than one-in-five perceive that they have been mistreated
because of that worldview are sobering, given that the HE sector has invested significantly
during the last decade in addressing harassment. Despite investment in specifically
religion-related harassment in 11 selected universities2, religion-related harassment,
                                              British Journal of Sociology of Education      15


mistreatment, insensitivity and silencing remain a problem, suggesting that there is a need
to ‘mainstream’ learning from those projects throughout the sector. That insensitive com-
ments come from staff as well as from students’ peers means university staffs require edu-
cation about how to tackle harassment and misunderstanding alongside enabling freedom
of expression for diverse religious and other worldviews. As the Higher Education (Freedom
of Speech) Act 2023 comes into force, the government, higher education regulator the Office
for Students, and other higher education bodies might also consider the broader range of
mechanisms available for preventing religion and belief-based harassment in the UK’s
universities.
    Our findings on equal opportunities appear the most positive of the three aspects of the
Equality Act. Students mostly feel that university is a safe space to express their worldview
and that staff accommodate their religious needs. It may be that universities have paid most
attention to this aspect of the Equality Act, for example by increasing provision for chaplains
and prayer spaces, as Aune, Guest, and Law’s (2019) findings show, with more universities
calling their chaplaincies ‘multi-faith’, and just over two-thirds having a permanent Muslim
prayer space. Such spaces may be contributing to the safety students feel at expressing their
worldview. Yet the fairly large numbers of students responding neither ‘agree’ nor ‘disagree’
to our equal opportunities questions suggest that while universities have improved their
practice, there is still work to do: perhaps students feel safer in some spaces than others,
and some staff support them to express their worldview while others do not.
    The focus on good relations presents a mixed picture: good relations have not been fully
achieved. Reassuringly, around three-in-five students do not feel silenced and four-in-five
have experienced a provocative encounter. However, just over one-in-five students experi-
ence silencing on an occasional or more frequent basis, and a similar number have not
experienced a provocative encounter. Further, we found evidence of some inequalities:
Christian students were more likely to feel silenced. Similarly, those attending traditional
elite institutions displayed a similar pattern: silencing was more commonplace than at other
institutions, but so were positive, provocative encounters.
    Why do students from minority worldviews report experiencing fewer provocative
encounters, particularly in the classroom, compared to Christian students? It may be the
case that, with increased awareness of the Equality Act, staff and students are slightly more
reticent to interrogate the religious views of those who are minoritised, and more willing
to challenge Christian students. While this, positively, means there is less insensitivity
expressed, and that staff provide opportunities for Christian students to have the provocative
encounters they need to develop positive attitudes to other religious groups (Peacock et al.
2023), it also means minoritised students have, and are given, fewer opportunities to engage
critically with their own worldviews. Universities should learn to create opportunities for
provocative encounters in and beyond the classroom, yet avoid these encounters becoming
opportunities for harassment, insensitivity or silencing, since research finds that harassment,
insensitivity and silencing are associated with a decline in students’ ability to regard those
with other beliefs positively (Mayhew, Rockenbach, and Dahl 2020). This is a particular
issue in elite universities, where greater opportunities for debate between faith groups might
contribute to an increase in negative experiences. Indeed, this may be one key unintended
consequence of the Equality Act’s efforts to enable communication and debate between
students of different protected characteristics: encouraging debate creates more opportunity
for misunderstanding and mistreatment, if debate becomes, or is interpreted as, harassment.
16      K. AUNE ET AL.


    Students’ religion or worldview makes a difference to how they experience university,
with non-religious students having the most positive experiences and religious students
the most negative. By religious group, Christian students are sometimes more likely to
report more negative treatment than students from minority religions, but concerning the
university accommodating religious holidays and other worldview-related observances,
students from minority religions are more likely to report negative treatment. Therefore,
universities should not assume that all religious students have the same experience, nor
should they think that negative treatment is something that only certain groups are subjected
to; the greater focus that student organisations have given to Islamophobia and antisemitism
may make universities less likely to see it as important to tackle other types of religion-related
discrimination. Negative experiences reported by non-religious students should not be
ignored, and the Equality Act’s language of ‘religion or belief ’ enables those whose beliefs
are not religious to have their experiences of prejudice taken seriously.
    Our findings about university type are also original. This article’s systematic examination
of the significance of university type offers a new direction for future analysis. At traditional
elite universities harassment, silencing and insensitive comments are much more common
than at Cathedrals Group or secular universities. This may be because of the style of teach-
ing, more debating, religious and political societies, and the higher proportion of privately
educated students schooled in the culture of debate, mockery and peer victimisation (von
Stumm and Plomin 2021). Students at elite institutions tend to be more economically priv-
ileged, with more time to spend debating ideas. But the one positive aspect of good relations
that is also more common at elite institutions is provocative encounters, where students
critically examine their worldview in the light of others’. As our typology reflects universities’
history (including religious history) in a way that other typologies (such as Mission Groups)
do not, it presents a promising direction for HE scholars studying inequalities obscured by
other approaches.
    Why haven’t the Equality Act’s intentions for religion or belief equality and good relations
been realised? Elgström’s work on the implementation of gender equality policies, intro-
duced above, is relevant. During implementation, ‘bureaucratic inertia’, a ‘lack of knowledge’
or ‘norm resistance’ (p.469) might have happened. Some university staff likely resisted the
Act, as there would be a conflict between the new norms and their previous practices and
values—for instance, the value of freedom of speech, academic freedom, or secularity (the
assumption that religion should be confined to the private sphere). Since Dinham and Jones’
study, more senior managers have probably come to regard religion as part of ‘an “equality
and diversity” brief ’ (Dinham and Jones 2012, 192), and so responsibility for religion may
be given to an EDI officer (who may not have religious expertise), a chaplain (who may not
have EDI expertise), HR or student services.
    Given the importance of ‘norm entrepreneurs’ at the start of the process of adopting
a new norm, who are these for religion or belief, and how influential are they? They
could be religious studies academics, chaplains or EDI officers. This likely varies
between different types of institutions. Traditional elite or Cathedrals Group universities
may have more influential norm entrepreneurs, with more prominent and better-funded
chaplains, who are sometimes managed by a member of senior management (Aune
et al. 2023). This may influence norm diffusion and could explain why we found some
increased evidence of religion or belief equality in these types of university—including
less mistreatment and insensitive comments at Cathedrals Group universities and more
                                              British Journal of Sociology of Education      17


provocative encounters at traditional elite universities. However, we also found coun-
tervailing trends: traditional elite universities had the most harassment, silencing and
insensitive comments, so the presence of influential religion or belief norm entrepre-
neurs can only be part of the explanation for universities adopting the norm of religion
or belief equality.
   There is also the role of students to consider. Just as Purdam et al. (2017) found that the
Equality Act received a lukewarm welcome from the religious constituents who would have
been assumed to champion it, it is likely that some students, individuals and groups, religious
or otherwise, do not further the Equality Act’s aims. For instance, an evangelical Christian
or Sunni Muslim group wishing to convert others to their religion might not give time to
forging interfaith relations, perceiving that as contrary to their mission. We would suggest
that this complex and incomplete process of norm implementation outlined by Elgström
helps explain why religion or belief equality has not been fully achieved, more than a decade
after the passage of the Equality Act. We can also apply Ahmed’s critiques of equality policies
to religion and belief equality and ask whether the creation of religion and belief documents
and plans—a responsibility of EDI officers and required by the Equality Act—conceals
problems of inequality and discrimination against religious staff and students. Perhaps time
and attention have been given to creating equality documentation that enables universities
to portray themselves as advancing equality rather than addressing the realities of religion
and belief harassment, discrimination or poor interfaith relations.
   Ahmed (2012, 17) takes these ideas further via the notion of institutional equality and
diversity policies as ‘non-performatives’, because ‘they do not bring about the effects they
name’. The equality laws culminating in the Equality Act brought about an ‘equality regime’
which may have been intended to overcome inequalities, but ‘can be an inequality regime
given new form, a set of processes that maintain what is supposedly being redressed’ (8).
Equality law carries with it an assumption that the very act of legislating achieves equality,
Ahmed argues, so that ‘having a policy becomes a substitute for action’ (2012, 11). The
wider context is the rise of audit or performance culture in higher education (Strathern
2000). Equality has become ‘embedded within performance culture’ (Ahmed 2012, 85),
making-documenting equality in a way that can be assessed a high priority, perhaps higher
than the actual achievement of equality. Increasingly, schemes such as Athena SWAN and
the Race Equality charter grade universities based on a long report documenting good
equality practice, provoking critiques that they are ‘a policy-scoring scheme’ (Armstrong
and Sullivan 2025) and not a rounded, nuanced assessment. Armstrong and Sullivan (2025,
235) also comment that, when considering Advance HE’s work advancing equality in higher
education, ‘The protected characteristics of age and religion or belief seem to be largely
ignored’.
   While Ahmed’s assessment, based on relatively small numbers of interviews with diversity
staff conducted before the Equality Act, is bleaker than ours, it demonstrates the complexity
of policy implementation in universities and how the prioritisation of documentary audit
measures has obvious limitations as a strategy for tackling inequalities. That such
‘paper-based’ measures have become more popular during a period of financial decline
across the HE sector is no accident; more complex interventions also have heavier resource
demands. Nevertheless, given the gap between policy and practice evidenced above, the
demand for qualitative research into equality solutions within universities could not be
clearer.

   Drawing on a large cross-university dataset, this article has been the first to examine
students’ experiences of religion or belief-related equality in universities in relation to the
Equality Act’s three foci (eliminating harassment; equal opportunities; and good relations).
Our analysis shows that despite the positioning of religion or belief as a protected charac-
teristic and diversity-friendly language such as ‘multi-faith’, inequalities remain, with some
religious students especially vulnerable to unequal treatment. We contend that the com-
plexity of policy implementation helps explain why religion or belief equality has not been
fully achieved.
   The limitations of this article are several. Our wider survey included many different
questions that could have been included as proxies for harassment, equal opportunities and
good relations; analysing responses to all of these would provide a fuller picture. Moreover,
the study from which this data was taken was not designed to measure the success of the
Equality Act, but was a wider study of how to build positive relationships between university
students across religion and worldview diversity. Finally, COVID-19 lockdowns are likely
to have impacted the experiences of some of the students surveyed, so their responses may
be shaped by the changes the pandemic brought to the student experience.
   Overall, the Equality Act appears to have supported students (and staff) in universities
to improve their provision for and support of students of diverse backgrounds, religions
and beliefs. Yet inequalities remain, especially in relation to harassment and good relations,
and for religious students. As the government shifted from Conservative to Labour in 2024,
the impacts of a complex policy climate on students of diverse religions and beliefs demand
further interrogation.
